Fractions homework has started more kitchen-table arguments than almost any other topic. I know, because parents tell me. Usually it goes like this: the child is confused, the parent tries to help using the method they learned thirty years ago, the child says "that's not how my teacher does it," and everyone ends up frustrated.

Here's the thing. You don't need to be good at fractions to help with fractions. You just need a few visual tricks and the right attitude. This post gives you both, ready to use tonight.

## First: it's not you, it's the topic

Fractions are genuinely weird the first time you meet them. Whole numbers behave nicely. Then suddenly 1/4 is smaller than 1/3 even though 4 is bigger than 3, and adding 1/2 + 1/3 definitely does not give 2/5. Of course kids get confused. The rules feel backwards until they click.

So if your child is struggling, that's normal. And if *you* feel rusty, that's normal too. You're not bad at math. You're just out of practice with something genuinely tricky.

## The pizza method (works for almost everything)

When in doubt, draw a pizza. Or a chocolate bar. Or anything divisible.

**What is a fraction?** Draw a circle, split it into 4 equal slices, shade 1. That's 1/4. The bottom number (denominator) says how many slices the whole is cut into. The top number (numerator) says how many slices we're talking about. Draw it once and the idea sticks far better than any definition.

**Why is 1/4 smaller than 1/3?** Draw two identical circles. Cut one into 4 slices, one into 3. Shade one slice of each. Now it's obvious: fewer slices means bigger slices. Kids who argue about this in words get it instantly in pictures.

**Adding fractions.** This is the big one. Draw 1/2 of a pizza and 1/4 of a pizza. To add them, you need same-size slices, so cut the half into two quarters. Now it's 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4. The drawing shows *why* you need a common denominator, which is the part kids usually memorize without understanding.

## The mistakes to watch for (gently)

There are three classic fraction errors. If you can spot them, you can help without doing the problem for them:

1. **Adding straight across.** 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5 is wrong, but it *looks* right to a kid. Ask: "can you draw both fractions and see if 2/5 makes sense?" The picture exposes it.
2. **Forgetting to simplify.** 2/4 is correct but unfinished; the answer should be 1/2. Teach them to always ask at the end: "can I make this simpler?"
3. **Flipping the wrong piece.** In division, kids flip the wrong fraction or forget to flip at all. The rule "flip the second one and multiply" is worth memorizing cold.

When you spot one, don't just correct it. Ask a question that leads them there: "what does the drawing say?" or "can that answer be right? 2/5 is smaller than 1/2, and you're *adding* something." Questions teach. Corrections just end the conversation.

## Three phrases that help (and three that don't)

Parents often say things with the best intentions that accidentally shut kids down. Small language tweaks make a big difference.

Instead of "this is easy," try "this is tricky at first." Calling it easy makes a struggling kid feel stupid. Calling it tricky makes them feel normal.

Instead of "no, that's wrong," try "interesting, walk me through that." You'll often discover their logic was *almost* right, and you can fix just the broken step.

Instead of "let me just do it," try "let's do the first step together, then you take it." The goal is a child who can do the next problem alone, not a finished worksheet.

## When to stop for the night

This matters more than any method. If you've been at it for 30 minutes and everyone's tense, stop. Write a short note to the teacher saying you worked on it together. Teachers respect that far more than a perfect worksheet that a parent clearly completed.

Tired brains don't learn fractions. Fresh ones do. Tomorrow is another day.
