Every parent I talk to asks some version of the same question: is it time for a tutor, or are we overreacting? Here is my honest answer as a teacher: most parents wait too long. By the time they call me, the child is not just behind, they are also convinced they are "bad at math," and that second problem is harder to fix than the first.

Math is a staircase. Each topic stands on the one below it. A small gap in fractions becomes a crisis in algebra, not because your child got dumber, but because the missing step finally got load-bearing. The earlier you catch it, the cheaper and faster the fix.

## The 7 signs

**1. Homework takes forever, or never starts.** If a 30-minute assignment regularly takes two hours, or if homework time starts with tears, stalling, or a mysterious sudden interest in cleaning their room, something is wrong. It is rarely laziness. Usually the child is stuck and does not know how to say so.

**2. "I was never good at math either" starts sounding true.** Listen for identity language: "I'm just not a math person." That sentence is a red flag, not a personality trait. It means the child has stopped trying to understand and started protecting themselves from feeling stupid. A good tutor's first job is demolishing that sentence.

**3. Grades dropped and stayed down.** One bad test is life. A full term of sliding grades, especially in math while other subjects hold steady, means a gap opened up and never closed. Math gaps do not self-heal. They compound.

**4. They can follow examples but not solve new problems.** This is the sneakiest one. Your child nods along in class, maybe does homework with examples visible, but freezes on anything slightly different. That means they memorized steps without understanding the idea. A tutor rebuilds the idea.

**5. You can't help anymore, and it's causing fights.** If homework help has become a nightly argument, stop. A tutor is cheaper than your relationship with your child. I mean that.

**6. A big transition is coming.** Moving to middle school, starting algebra, facing entrance exams. Transitions multiply existing gaps. Getting a tutor *before* the hard term starts is one of the highest-value moves a parent can make.

**7. They ask for help.** Some kids actually say it: "I don't get math, can someone explain it to me?" Believe them immediately. A child asking for help is handing you a gift. Do not make them ask twice.

If two or more of these sound familiar, it is time. Do not wait for the report card to confirm it.

## What to look for in a tutor

Not all tutoring is equal. Here is what actually matters:

- **They diagnose before they teach.** A good tutor's first session is mostly questions: where exactly did it break? Anyone who starts teaching without finding the gap is guessing.
- **They teach understanding, not tricks.** Tricks fade. "Just remember to flip it" helps on Tuesday and hurts on the final. Ask the tutor how they would explain *why* something works. If they cannot, keep looking.
- **Your child likes them.** This is not soft stuff. A child who dreads the sessions will not learn in them. The first session should end with your child feeling smarter, not smaller.
- **They give you a plan.** "We will meet twice a week" is not a plan. "We will rebuild fractions over three weeks, then tackle the algebra unit" is a plan. Ask for one.

Red flags: guarantees of specific grades, doing the homework *for* the child, no questions about what is actually wrong, or sessions that are just more worksheets with no explanation.

## What should it cost?

Prices vary wildly by place and format, so treat these as rough guides, not quotes. Group and online sessions usually cost the least. One-on-one costs more, with experienced specialists at the top.

Here is what matters more than the hourly rate: **how many sessions until you see a change.** A cheap tutor who needs six months costs more than a good one who fixes the gap in six weeks. Ask upfront: how will we know this is working, and by when?

Also know that tutoring is a bridge, not a lifestyle. The goal is a child who outgrows the tutor. If a tutor seems comfortable with endless sessions and no end date, that is a business model, not a teaching plan.

## What you can do right now, for free

While you are deciding, do the single most useful diagnostic there is: have your child explain a recent homework problem to *you*, out loud, step by step. Where they stumble or go vague, that is the gap. Write it down and hand it to any tutor you interview. You will instantly separate the good ones from the guessers.

And if the problem is really math anxiety rather than a knowledge gap, read [why math feels hard](/blog/beat-math-anxiety) first. Sometimes the block is emotional, and no amount of extra practice fixes an emotional block.

## The bottom line

Getting a tutor is not admitting defeat, yours or your child's. It is recognizing that math is a staircase and hiring someone to find the missing step. The parents I respect most are not the ones whose kids never struggled. They are the ones who noticed early and acted.
