Fractions and decimals are the same idea wearing different clothes. 1/2 and 0.5 are the same amount. So why do we have both? Because each one is better at certain jobs. Knowing which to reach for, and how to switch between them, is one of those quiet skills that makes everything else easier.

I tell my students: fractions are for thinking, decimals are for measuring. Let's unpack that.

## When fractions win

Fractions are exact. 1/3 is exactly one third, no rounding, no approximation. Write it as a decimal and you get 0.333..., trailing off forever. The fraction tells the truth. The decimal has to lie a little.

This matters whenever precision counts. In recipes, 1/3 cup is a real measurement; 0.33 cups is an apology. In algebra, keeping 2/3 as a fraction through five steps of working gives you a clean answer at the end. Converting to 0.67 early means every step after that carries a small error, and small errors grow.

Fractions are also better for *seeing* relationships. 3/4 versus 2/3: cross-multiply (3 × 3 = 9, 4 × 2 = 8) and 3/4 is bigger. Try seeing that in 0.75 versus 0.666... It's there, but it's murkier.

Rule of thumb: if you're working through steps on paper, especially with division that doesn't come out even, stay in fractions.

## When decimals win

Decimals are built for the real world of measuring and money. Nobody prices a shirt at $19 3/4. It's $19.75, because cash registers and rulers speak decimal.

Comparing is also easier in decimals when the numbers are close. Is 0.47 or 0.471 bigger? Line up the digits: 0.470 vs 0.471, so the second. Try that with 47/100 vs 471/1000 and you'll be cross-multiplying for a while.

And calculators? They only speak decimal. The moment you pick up a calculator, you're in decimal land whether you like it or not.

Rule of thumb: money, measurements, and final answers for "real world" questions usually want decimals.

## Converting: fraction to decimal

Divide the top by the bottom. That's the whole method.

1/4: 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25. Done.

3/8: 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375. Done.

Some fractions never terminate: 1/3 = 0.333..., 2/11 = 0.1818... When that happens, you either round (0.33) or keep the fraction. Now you know why the previous section exists.

Worth memorizing cold, because they come up constantly: 1/2 = 0.5, 1/4 = 0.25, 3/4 = 0.75, 1/5 = 0.2, 1/8 = 0.125. Honestly, just learn these five. They'll pay you back hundreds of times.

## Converting: decimal to fraction

This direction trips more people up, but it's mechanical:

1. Write the decimal over its place value. 0.75 is 75 hundredths, so 75/100.
2. Simplify. 75/100 divides by 25 to give 3/4.

Another: 0.6 = 6/10 = 3/5. And 0.125 = 125/1000 = 1/8 (divide top and bottom by 125).

The key step everyone forgets is simplifying at the end. 75/100 is technically correct, but 3/4 is the answer your teacher wants. Always ask: can I make this smaller?

## The hybrid strategy (what good students actually do)

Here's what strong math students do without thinking about it: they switch mid-problem.

Start in fractions for the exact working. A problem like "1/3 of 90 plus 1/4 of 90" is easy in fractions: 30 + 22.5... wait, that 22.5 is a decimal sneaking in. Fine. That's the point. Nobody is grading your purity. Use fractions where they're clean, decimals where they're convenient, and convert at whatever step makes life easiest.

The one rule: convert deliberately, not accidentally. Know which form you're in and why.

## Quick decision guide

- **Exact answer needed, division involved?** Fractions.
- **Money, measurements, calculator work?** Decimals.
- **Comparing two close values?** Whichever form they're already in, or decimals.
- **Showing your working step by step?** Fractions, then convert at the end if needed.
- **Answer looks like 0.333...?** Go back to the fraction. It's trying to tell you something.

Learn both directions of conversion until they're boring. That's when you know you've got them. And when in doubt, the [fraction calculator](/calculators/fractions) will show you both forms side by side with every step explained.
