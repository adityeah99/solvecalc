Math is not a subject you can cram the night before. You cannot memorize your way through it the way you might with a list of dates. But you can study it well, and a good plan makes a real difference. Here is one that works.

## Start by finding out what is actually on the test

Before you open a single book, figure out the topics. Check your notes, ask your teacher, or look at the chapter titles you covered.

Write each topic on its own line. Then, next to each one, mark how you feel: a check if you are solid, a question mark if you are shaky, an X if you are lost. This turns a scary blur into a short, honest list.

## Spend your time where it is shaky

Most students study what they already know, because it feels good. That is a trap. Your check-marked topics can wait.

Put your time into the question marks and X's. If three topics need work and you have two hours, that is about 40 minutes each, with 20 minutes left to review the whole thing. Five minutes of planning saves you from drifting.

## Practice by doing, not by reading

Reading a worked example feels like studying, but it is closer to watching someone else ride a bike. You learn math by solving problems with your own hand.

Redo problems from your homework and class notes with the answers covered. Then check. When you get one wrong, do not just glance at the solution and nod. Work out where your version went off.

A common slip is the **order of operations**. In 2 + 3 × 4, you multiply first, so the answer is 14, not 20. If that rule ever trips you up, the [order of operations article](/articles/order-of-operations) walks through it slowly.

## Make a one-page cheat sheet (even if you cannot bring it)

Squeeze the whole unit onto a single page: the key formulas, one worked example of each type, and the mistakes you keep making.

The act of choosing what belongs on that page forces you to decide what matters. Many students find they barely need the sheet by the time they finish writing it.

## Test yourself under real conditions

A day or two before, set a timer and work a set of mixed problems with no notes. Mixed matters: on the real test, problems do not arrive grouped by topic, so you need practice deciding *which* method to use.

Notice which problems make your stomach drop. Those are your last-minute priorities.

## The night before

- Do a light review of your cheat sheet. Do not learn anything brand new.
- Pack your calculator, pencils, and eraser.
- Sleep. A tired brain forgets steps it knew perfectly the day before.

Trying to learn a new topic at 11 p.m. usually costs you more in tiredness than it gives you in knowledge.

## During the test

Read each question twice before you write. Do the ones you know first to bank easy points and settle your nerves.

If you get stuck, circle it and move on. Come back with fresh eyes. And when you finish early, do not leave. Recheck your arithmetic and make sure you answered what was actually asked.

## A quick weekly habit

The students who find tests easy usually are not studying harder the night before. They are doing a little every week: redoing two or three tricky problems, keeping notes tidy, asking about anything confusing while it is still fresh.

Twenty minutes on a normal Tuesday is worth more than three panicked hours the night before.

## Try this next

Pick your shakiest topic and take a short [quiz](/quizzes/algebra) on it, or work through a [lesson](/learn/algebra) to fill the gap before test day. Then keep every wrong answer you find and study it, because those mistakes are the fastest map to a better grade.
