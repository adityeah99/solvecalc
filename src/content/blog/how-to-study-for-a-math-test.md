I bombed my first big algebra test. Not a little. Properly bombed it. I had "studied" for hours, which meant I had stared at my notes for hours. There's a difference, and this post is about that difference.

Math isn't a subject you can cram the night before. You can't memorize your way through it the way you might with a list of dates. But you can study it well. Here's the plan I wish someone had handed me back then.

## First, find out what is actually on the test

Before you open a single book, figure out the topics. Check your notes, ask your teacher, or look at the chapter titles you covered.

Write each topic on its own line. Then, next to each one, mark how you feel: a check if you're solid, a question mark if you're shaky, an X if you're lost.

This turns a scary blur into a short, honest list.

## Spend your time where it's shaky

Here's the trap almost everyone falls into: studying what you already know, because it feels good. Comforting, but a waste of your evening.

Your check-marked topics can wait. Put your time into the question marks and X's. If three topics need work and you've two hours, that's about 40 minutes each, with 20 minutes left to review. Five minutes of planning saves you from drifting.

## Practice by doing, not by reading

Reading a worked example feels like studying. It's closer to watching someone else ride a bike. You learn math by solving problems with your own hand.

Redo problems from your homework and class notes with the answers covered. Then check. When you get one wrong, don't just glance at the solution and nod. Work out where your version went off, step by step.

A classic slip is the **order of operations**. In 2 + 3 × 4, you multiply first, so the answer is 14, not 20. If that rule ever trips you up, the [order of operations article](/articles/order-of-operations) walks through it slowly.

## Make a one-page cheat sheet (even if you can't bring it)

Squeeze the whole unit onto a single page: the key formulas, one worked example of each type, and the mistakes you keep making.

Here's the secret: the sheet itself barely matters. Choosing what belongs on that page forces you to decide what matters. Many students find they barely need the sheet by the time they finish writing it. The writing was the studying.

## Test yourself under real conditions

A day or two before the test, set a timer and work a set of mixed problems with no notes. Mixed matters: on the real test, problems don't arrive grouped by topic. You need practice deciding *which* method to use.

Notice which problems make your stomach drop. Those are your last-minute priorities.

## The night before: wind down, don't cram

- Do a light review of your cheat sheet. Don't learn anything brand new.
- Pack your calculator, pencils, and eraser.
- Sleep. A tired brain forgets steps it knew perfectly the day before.

Trying to learn a new topic at 11 p.m. usually costs you more in tiredness than it gives you in knowledge.

## During the test: play it smart

Read each question twice before you write.

Do the ones you know first. Bank the easy points, settle your nerves, build momentum. Then go back for the hard ones.

If you get stuck, circle it and move on. Come back with fresh eyes. And when you finish early, don't leave. Recheck your arithmetic and make sure you answered what was actually asked.

## The habit that makes tests easy

The students who find tests easy usually aren't studying harder the night before. They're doing a little every week: redoing two or three tricky problems, keeping notes tidy, asking about anything confusing while it's still fresh.

Twenty minutes on a normal Tuesday is worth more than three panicked hours the night before.

## Try this next

Pick your shakiest topic and take a short [quiz](/quizzes/algebra) on it, or work through a [lesson](/learn/algebra) to fill the gap before test day. Then keep every wrong answer you find and study it, because those mistakes are the fastest map to a better grade.
