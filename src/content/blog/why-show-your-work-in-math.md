"But I did it in my head." I hear this every year, usually right after I hand back a test. The student got the wrong answer, showed no working, and got zero marks for a question they half-understood. It drives me slightly mad, because the fix is so simple: write it down.

Showing your work isn't about pleasing the teacher. It's a strategy. A good one. Here's why.

## Reason 1: partial credit is real money

Most math tests don't grade like a light switch. They grade like a staircase. A five-mark question might give one mark for setting up the equation, two for the correct method, one for accurate arithmetic, and one for the final answer.

That means a wrong final answer with good working can still earn three or four marks out of five. A wrong final answer with no working earns zero. Same wrong answer, completely different result.

I once watched a student go from a D to a B on a single exam just by writing steps. Her math didn't get better overnight. Her mark collection did. She was leaving marks on the table before, and she stopped.

Think of each written step as a little insurance policy on your grade.

## Reason 2: your brain lies to you (mine does too)

Mental math feels fast and clever. It is also where most errors hide. When you do everything in your head, a sign slip or a misread number vanishes without a trace. You can't find what you can't see.

Written steps make your thinking visible. And visible thinking can be checked. When you finish a problem, you can walk back through your steps like footprints and spot exactly where things went sideways.

Here's a classic: solve 3x - 7 = 11. In your head, you might jump to x = 6 and feel confident. On paper: add 7 to both sides (3x = 18), divide by 3 (x = 6). Same answer, but now if you'd accidentally written 3x = 4, you'd see it staring at you. In your head, that error is invisible.

The [linear equations walkthrough](/articles/how-to-solve-a-linear-equation) shows what proper step-by-step working looks like for every equation type. Copy that style until it becomes habit.

## Reason 3: hard problems become possible

Here's something nobody tells you: the real purpose of showing work isn't the easy questions. It's the hard ones.

Easy questions fit in your head. Hard ones don't. A multi-step word problem or a tricky equation has too many moving parts to juggle mentally. Writing steps down is like clearing desk space. Each step you write is one thing you no longer have to hold in your memory, which frees your brain to think about the next move.

This is why students who "show work" suddenly find they can do harder problems. They didn't get smarter. They gave their working memory some help.

## Reason 4: it teaches you to think

There's a deeper benefit that pays off for years. Writing out your reasoning forces you to be precise. Vague ideas can't survive on paper. When you write "subtract 2x from both sides," you have to actually understand what that means, not just feel it.

Over time, this habit builds something valuable: the ability to explain your own thinking. That skill shows up everywhere, from science labs to job interviews to arguing about a bill at a restaurant.

## What "showing work" actually looks like

Students sometimes think it means writing an essay. It doesn't. Good working is short and clear:

1. Write the original problem down.
2. Write each new step on its own line.
3. Keep equals signs lined up so both sides stay visible.
4. Box or underline the final answer.
5. If you check your answer, write the check too.

That's it. Nobody wants paragraphs. They want a trail they can follow.

## The objections (and why they fail)

**"It takes too long."** It takes about ten extra seconds per question. One partial-credit mark repays that investment many times over.

**"I can do it in my head."** Maybe, for easy ones. But exams aren't a performance. They're mark collection. Write it down.

**"My working is messy."** Messy working still earns marks and still catches errors. Tidy is nice, but optional. Written beats mental every time.

**"The teacher knows I understand."** The teacher can't grade what they can't see. And more importantly, *you* can't check what you can't see either.

## Start today

Next homework set, write every step. All of them, even the obvious ones. It will feel slow for about a week. Then it becomes automatic, and your marks will tell you the rest.

Your future self, staring at a tricky exam question with a blank page, will thank you for the habit.
