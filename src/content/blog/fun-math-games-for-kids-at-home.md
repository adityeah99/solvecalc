The best math practice does not look like practice. I have watched kids who groan at worksheets beg for "one more round" of a dice game that drills the exact same arithmetic. The math did not change. The packaging did. Here are five games you can play tonight with stuff already in your house.

## 1. Dice wars (addition and multiplication)

You need: two dice (or one die rolled twice).

Each player rolls both dice, adds them up (younger kids) or multiplies them (older kids). Highest total wins the round. First to ten round-wins takes the game.

Why it works: it is fast, it is competitive, and the repetition is invisible because the child is focused on winning, not drilling. My students who play this at home learn their multiplication facts noticeably faster, and they do not even realize they are studying. Start with addition for ages 5 to 7, switch to multiplication around age 8.

Twist for older kids: use three dice and multiply all three, or roll two dice and make the biggest two-digit number you can, then compare.

## 2. The price is right (estimation)

You need: nothing. Play it anywhere.

Point at anything, a chair, a parked car, the groceries in your cart, and everyone guesses the price. Closest guess wins. Then talk about *why* people guessed what they did.

Why it works: estimation is one of the most underrated math skills, and it is almost never taught directly. Kids learn to anchor guesses to things they know ("well, the small milk is $3, so the big one is probably around $5"). That anchoring instinct is the foundation of number sense. Play it in shops, in traffic, on walks. Zero setup, infinite rounds.

## 3. Card countdown (mental arithmetic)

You need: a deck of cards (remove face cards, or assign them values).

Flip two cards. Players race to add, subtract, or multiply them, you choose the operation based on what needs practice. First correct answer takes the cards. Most cards at the end wins.

Why it works: the time pressure is gentle because it comes from a sibling or parent, not a test. Kids who freeze on timed worksheets often thrive here because the game frame makes speed feel fun instead of scary. For a gentler version, skip the racing and just take turns. The [times tables article](/articles/times-tables) pairs perfectly with the multiplication version.

## 4. Kitchen fractions (real cooking)

You need: a recipe and a measuring cup.

Ask your child to double the recipe. Or halve it. Or make one and a half times. Suddenly 1/2 cup doubled is a real question with a real answer they can check by measuring.

Why it works: fractions are the topic kids understand least and memorize most. Cooking makes them concrete. When a child has physically poured half of a half cup, the abstract idea of 1/4 stops being abstract. Bonus: you get cookies. I have never seen a worksheet produce cookies.

Start simple: doubling is just addition. Halving introduces real fraction thinking. Let them get it wrong and check with the measuring cup. The cup does not judge.

## 5. Twenty questions: number edition (logic)

You need: nothing.

Think of a number between 1 and 100. Your child asks yes-or-no questions to find it. "Is it bigger than 50?" "Is it even?" Count how many questions it takes, then swap roles and try to beat their score.

Why it works: this is secretly a lesson in halving and ranges, which is the intuition behind everything from estimation to binary search. Kids who play this start asking "is it bigger than 75?" instead of guessing randomly, and that shift, from guessing to strategizing, is mathematical thinking in its purest form.

## A few ground rules that make games work

- **Keep it short.** Fifteen minutes of a game beats an hour of groaning. Stop while it is still fun.
- **Lose gracefully, sometimes.** Let them win enough to stay hooked, but not so much they never lose. Losing is where the learning hides.
- **Talk math out loud.** "I knew 7 times 8 is 56, so I..." Hearing you think out loud teaches more than any explanation. Model the thinking, not just the answer.
- **Never turn the game into a test.** The moment you start grading the game, it stops being a game. If they get something wrong, just play the round again.
