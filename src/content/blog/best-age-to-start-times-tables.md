Every parent asks me this eventually, usually with a worried look: "Should my six-year-old know their times tables by now?" The short answer is no. The longer answer is more useful, so here it is.

Most children are ready for times tables somewhere between ages 7 and 9. But birthdays are a rough guide at best. Readiness signs matter far more than the calendar, and pushing too early can do real damage. Let me explain.

## Why rushing backfires

I once worked with a seven-year-old whose parents had drilled the 7 times table into her for weeks. She could recite it perfectly, like a poem. Then I asked her what 7 × 4 *means*, and she stared at me blankly. She'd memorized sounds, not math.

That's the danger of starting too early. Before a child understands multiplication as "groups of things," times tables are just chanting. The chanting might impress grandparents, but it builds nothing underneath. And when school moves on to division or fractions, the hollow foundation cracks.

Worse, early pressure can attach anxiety to the whole topic. A child who was forced to perform before they were ready often decides they're "bad at math." That label sticks around far longer than any times table.

## The readiness signs (more important than age)

Forget the birthday. Look for these instead:

1. **They can add fluently.** If 8 + 7 still takes real effort, multiplication will be a wall. Addition should feel easy first.
2. **They understand "groups of."** Ask: "if there are 3 bags with 4 apples each, how many apples?" If they can work it out by adding (4 + 4 + 4), they're ready. The times table just speeds up what they already understand.
3. **They can skip-count.** 2, 4, 6, 8... and 5, 10, 15, 20... Skip counting is multiplication wearing casual clothes. If they can do it, the tables are close.
4. **They're curious, not scared.** A child who asks "what's 6 times 7?" is ready to learn the answer. A child who groans when numbers come up needs more time, not more pressure.

If you see two or three of these signs, go ahead. If not, wait a few months and check again. Nothing is lost by waiting. Plenty is lost by rushing.

## The no-pressure approach that actually works

Here's the method I recommend to every parent. It works because it never feels like a test.

**Start with the easy wins.** The 1, 2, 5, and 10 tables have obvious patterns. Master those first and your child feels successful immediately. Confidence is fuel.

**Learn in a smart order, not 1 to 12.** After the easy ones, do 3 and 4, then use tricks for 6, 7, 8 (like "if you know 5 × 7, just add one more 7"). Save 11 and 12 for last. There's no rule that says you must go in order.

**Five minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday.** Short, regular practice builds memory. Long, rare sessions build resentment. Stick a few facts on the fridge. Ask one in the car. Keep it light.

**Use it for real.** "We're buying 4 packs of juice with 6 boxes each, how many is that?" Real uses make tables feel like tools, not chores.

**Never time them until they're confident.** Timed drills too early teach one lesson: being slow means being dumb. That's false, and it's poisonous. Speed comes naturally with confidence. Let it.

## What if they're "behind"?

Maybe the class has moved on and your child hasn't. First, breathe. Times tables are a skill, not a deadline. Plenty of strong mathematicians learned them late.

Second, go back to the readiness signs. Fill the actual gap, which is usually shaky addition or the "groups of" idea, not the tables themselves. Fix the foundation and the tables follow quickly.

Third, talk to the teacher. Ask what the class is doing and where your child stands. Teachers see this constantly and usually have a plan. You're a team.

## Keep it joyful if you can

The kids who learn tables fastest are the ones having some fun with them. Card games, dice games, silly songs, our [times tables guide](/blog/times-tables-the-easy-way) with its patterns and tricks. If everyone's laughing, everyone's learning.

And if today isn't the day, that's fine too. The tables will wait. Childhood won't.
