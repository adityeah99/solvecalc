Algebra exams scare people because algebra feels like everything at once. Letters, brackets, graphs, word problems. But here's what I tell my students every year: an algebra exam is really just five or six topics wearing a trench coat. Learn the topics one at a time, and the exam stops being scary.

I learned this the hard way. My first algebra exam, I "studied" by rereading the whole textbook front to back. Twice. I got a C minus and felt lucky. The next time, I studied topic by topic instead, and it felt like a different subject entirely.

## Step 1: list the topics (this takes 15 minutes)

Get your syllabus, your chapter list, or just flip through your notebook. Write down every topic as its own line. A typical algebra exam list looks like this:

1. Simplifying expressions (collecting like terms)
2. Solving one-step and two-step equations
3. Equations with brackets and fractions
4. Inequalities (the < and > kind)
5. Graphing straight lines
6. Word problems (translating English into algebra)

Yours might differ. That's fine. The point is to see the whole battlefield before you start fighting.

Now rate each topic honestly: a check if you could do it right now, a question mark if you're shaky, an X if you'd freeze. Be brutal. Nobody sees this list but you.

## Step 2: attack the X's first

Most students do the opposite. They start with what they know because it feels productive. It's not. It's procrastination wearing a study costume.

Start with your worst topic. Give it a full, focused session before you touch anything else. Why? The worst topic has the most room to improve, so every hour there buys you the most marks. And everything after it feels easier by comparison, which keeps you going.

A rough split that works: spend half your total study time on X topics, a third on question-mark topics, and the rest reviewing the checks.

## Step 3: practice in three passes

This is the part most students skip, and it's the part that actually works. For each topic, do three passes:

**Pass 1: copy and understand.** Work through two or three solved examples slowly. Cover the solution, try each step yourself, then uncover and compare. If your step differs, figure out why before moving on.

**Pass 2: do it cold.** Close the book. Do five problems of that type from scratch. No peeking. This is where the learning happens, and yes, it feels harder. That's the point.

**Pass 3: mix it up.** This is the secret weapon. Do problems from different topics jumbled together, like the real exam. On test day, nobody tells you "this is a brackets question." You have to recognize the type yourself, and that recognition only comes from mixed practice.

If equations are your weak spot, the [linear equations article](/articles/how-to-solve-a-linear-equation) walks through every type with steps, and you can check your working in the [equation solver](/calculators/equation).

## Step 4: build your formula sheet

One page. That's all you get. The rules of the game: key formulas, one example of each type, and your personal mistake list.

The mistake list is the most valuable part. Every time you get a practice problem wrong, write down *what went wrong* in five words or less: "forgot to flip inequality sign", "multiplied only first bracket term", "sign error moving terms". Before the exam, read that list. It is a map of exactly where your marks leak out.

A student of mine once told me her mistake list was worth "at least ten marks" on the real thing. I believe her.

## Step 5: the final 48 hours

Two days before: one full timed practice, mixed topics, no notes. Grade it harshly. Whatever you got wrong becomes your final review list.

The day before: light review only. Read your formula sheet. Skim your mistake list. Do three or four easy problems to stay warm, then stop and sleep.

The morning of: eat something, bring spare pens, and arrive early enough that you're not rushing. Rushing burns the exact mental energy you need for the first ten minutes of the exam.

## During the exam

Read every question twice. I mean it. Half of all "I knew this" mistakes are actually "I misread this" mistakes.

Do a quick first pass and answer everything you find easy. This banks marks and calms you down. Then go back for the harder ones with your full brain.

Show every step of your working, even when you could do it in your head. Steps earn partial credit, and they let you spot your own errors when you check. If you finish early, don't leave. Recheck the questions you flagged, and verify each answer by plugging it back in.

## One last thing

Algebra rewards consistency more than brilliance. The students who do well aren't the ones with some special math gene. They're the ones who practiced a little, regularly, and fixed their mistakes instead of hiding from them.

Start with your X topics today. Future you will be glad you did.
