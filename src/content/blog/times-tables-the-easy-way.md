The times tables look like a wall of numbers to memorize. 144 facts, all staring at you.

Here's what I tell every student who panics at that wall: most of it's easier than it seems, because the facts are full of patterns. Learn the patterns, and the memorizing shrinks to almost nothing.

## You already know half of them

First shortcut, and it's a big one: order doesn't matter in multiplication. 6 × 7 gives the same answer as 7 × 6, which is 42.

The moment you learn 6 × 7, you also know 7 × 6 for free. Once you notice this, the number of facts you actually have to learn drops by roughly half.

## Four tables you barely need to memorize

Some tables are patterns wearing a disguise.

- **The 1 times table** leaves every number unchanged. 1 × 8 = 8. Done.
- **The 2 times table** is just doubling: 2 × 7 is 7 + 7 = 14.
- **The 10 times table** adds a zero: 10 × 6 = 60.
- **The 5 times table** always ends in 5 or 0, and every answer is half of the matching 10s fact. Since 10 × 8 = 80, we know 5 × 8 = 40.

That's four whole rows handled by patterns, not memory. The wall keeps shrinking.

## The 9s have a beautiful trick

The 9s look hard but hide a neat pattern. In every answer up to 9 × 10, the two digits add up to 9, and the tens digit is one less than the number you multiplied by.

Take 9 × 7. One less than 7 is 6, so the tens digit is 6. The digits must sum to 9, and 6 + 3 = 9, so the ones digit is 3. The answer is **63**.

There's a finger version too. Hold up both hands and bend down your 7th finger from the left. You've 6 fingers to its left and 3 to its right: 63 again.

## The 4s are just doubling, twice

To multiply by 4, double the number and then double again, because 4 is 2 × 2.

For 4 × 7: double 7 to get 14, then double 14 to get **28**. Two easy steps beat one memorized fact.

## The handful actually worth memorizing

After all those patterns, only a few "middle" facts are left to learn the plain way. The one most students trip on is 7 × 8 = **56**. A silly memory hook helps: 5, 6, 7, 8, so "56 = 7 × 8." Say it out loud a few times. It sticks because it's ridiculous.

The others in this small group, like 6 × 7 = 42 and 6 × 8 = 48, are few enough to drill in a week. That's the whole remaining wall. A week.

## How to practice so it actually sticks

Short and often beats long and rare. Five minutes a day teaches your brain far more than an hour on Sunday.

- Practice the table you find hardest first, while you're fresh.
- Say the facts out loud. Speaking uses more of your memory than reading.
- Mix them up once you feel steady. Real problems don't arrive in order, so your practice shouldn't either.

Knowing which numbers multiply to give a target also helps with [factors and multiples](/articles/factors-and-multiples) later, so this practice pays off well beyond the tables themselves.

## Why this matters more than you think

Solid times tables are the quiet engine behind fractions, division, ratios, and early algebra. When the basic facts are automatic, your brain has room to think about the actual problem instead of grinding out 7 × 8 halfway through.

That's the real reason to get them down now. Everything after them gets lighter.
