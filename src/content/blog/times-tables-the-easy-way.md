The times tables can look like a wall of numbers to memorize. But most of that wall is easier than it seems, because the facts are full of patterns. Learn the patterns, and the memorizing shrinks to almost nothing.

## You already know half of them

Here is the first shortcut: order does not matter in multiplication. 6 × 7 gives the same answer as 7 × 6, which is 42.

That means the moment you learn 6 × 7, you also know 7 × 6 for free. Once you notice this, the number of facts you actually have to learn drops by roughly half. That alone should make the whole thing feel lighter.

## The easy tables, in order

Some tables barely need memorizing at all.

- **The 1 times table** leaves every number unchanged. 1 × 8 = 8.
- **The 2 times table** is just doubling: 2 × 7 is 7 + 7 = 14.
- **The 10 times table** adds a zero: 10 × 6 = 60.
- **The 5 times table** always ends in 5 or 0, and every answer is half of the matching 10s fact. Since 10 × 8 = 80, we know 5 × 8 = 40.

That is four whole rows handled by patterns, not memory.

## The 9 times table has a beautiful trick

The 9s look hard but hide a neat pattern. In every answer up to 9 × 10, the two digits add up to 9, and the tens digit is one less than the number you multiplied by.

Take 9 × 7. One less than 7 is 6, so the tens digit is 6. The digits must sum to 9, and 6 + 3 = 9, so the ones digit is 3. The answer is **63**.

There is a finger version too. Hold up both hands and bend down your 7th finger from the left. You have 6 fingers to its left and 3 to its right: 63 again.

## The 4 times table is just double, twice

To multiply by 4, double the number and then double again, because 4 is 2 × 2.

For 4 × 7: double 7 to get 14, then double 14 to get **28**. Two easy steps beat one memorized fact for a lot of people.

## The handful that are genuinely worth memorizing

After all those patterns, only a few "middle" facts are left to learn the plain way. The one most students trip on is 7 × 8 = **56**. A silly memory hook helps: 5, 6, 7, 8, so "56 = 7 × 8."

The others in this small group, like 6 × 7 = 42 and 6 × 8 = 48, are few enough to drill in a week.

## How to practice so it sticks

Short and often beats long and rare. Five minutes a day teaches your brain far more than an hour on Sunday.

- Practice the table you find hardest first, while you are fresh.
- Say the facts out loud. Speaking uses more of your memory than reading.
- Mix them up once you feel steady. Real problems do not arrive in order.

Knowing which numbers multiply to give a target also helps with [factors and multiples](/articles/factors-and-multiples) later, so this practice pays off well beyond the tables themselves.

## Why this matters later

Solid times tables are the quiet engine behind fractions, division, ratios, and early algebra. When the basic facts are automatic, your brain has room to think about the actual problem instead of grinding out 7 × 8 halfway through.

That is the real reason to get them down now. It makes everything after them lighter.
