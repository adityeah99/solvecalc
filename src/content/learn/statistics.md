Statistics is about making sense of a list of numbers. Instead of staring at every value, you find one or two numbers that sum up the whole list.

In this lesson we'll use one small set of data. Sam timed his walk to school every day for a week. Here are his times in minutes:

**12, 15, 11, 14, 15, 13, 18**

Let's see what these seven numbers can tell us.

## Mean: the "fair share" average

When most people say "average", they mean the **mean**. To find it, add up all the values, then divide by how many values there are.

For Sam's walks:

- Total: 12 + 15 + 11 + 14 + 15 + 13 + 18 = 98
- Number of values: 7
- Mean: 98 ÷ 7 = **14 minutes**

You can think of the mean as a fair share. If all the minutes were shared out equally across the 7 days, each day would get 14 minutes.

## Median: the middle value

The **median** is the middle value once the numbers are in order. Always sort them first, from smallest to largest.

Sam's times in order: 11, 12, 13, **14**, 15, 15, 18

There are 7 values, so the middle one is the 4th. The median is **14 minutes**.

If there's an even number of values, there are two in the middle. Take the mean of those two. For 3, 8, 5, 10, sort them to get 3, 5, 8, 10. The middle two are 5 and 8, so the median is (5 + 8) ÷ 2 = **6.5**.

## Mode: the most common value

The **mode** is the value that appears most often. In Sam's list, 15 appears twice and every other time appears once. So the mode is **15 minutes**.

A list can have more than one mode if there's a tie. For example, 2, 2, 5, 7, 7 has two modes: 2 and 7. If every value appears the same number of times, there is no mode.

The mode is the only average that works for things that aren't numbers. If you ask your class to name their favorite fruit, the mode might be "apple".

## Range: how spread out the data is

The mean, median and mode tell you about the middle of the data. The **range** tells you how spread out it is. To find it, subtract the smallest value from the largest.

For Sam: 18 − 11 = **7 minutes**.

A small range means the values are close together. A big range means they're spread far apart. Sam's walks are fairly steady, since his fastest and slowest days are only 7 minutes apart.

## Which average should you use?

Most of the time, the mean works well. But it has a weak spot: one very big or very small value can drag it a long way.

Imagine five friends get this much pocket money each week: $5, $6, $6, $7 and $46.

- Mean: (5 + 6 + 6 + 7 + 46) ÷ 5 = 70 ÷ 5 = **$14**
- Median: the middle of 5, 6, 6, 7, 46 is **$6**

Four of the five friends get $7 or less, so saying "the average is $14" gives the wrong idea. The median, $6, paints a much fairer picture. A value that sits far away from the rest, like the $46 here, is called an **outlier**.

A simple rule of thumb:

- Use the **mean** when the numbers are fairly even, with no big outliers.
- Use the **median** when there are outliers, like with house prices or incomes.
- Use the **mode** when you want the most popular choice.

It's often worth finding more than one. If the mean and median are close, the data is probably fairly balanced. If they're far apart, look for an outlier.
