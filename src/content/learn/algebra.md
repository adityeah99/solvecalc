Algebra looks strange at first because of all the letters. But the big idea is simple: a letter is just a stand-in for a number we don't know yet.

If you have ever thought "I have $12 and the game costs $20, so I need $8 more," you have already done algebra in your head. This lesson shows you how to write that kind of thinking down and solve it step by step.

## Variables: letters that stand for numbers

A **variable** is a letter that holds the place of a number. We often use x, but any letter works. Sometimes it helps to pick a letter that reminds you what it means, like c for cost or t for time.

Say a cinema ticket costs t dollars. If you buy 3 tickets, the total is 3 × t. In algebra we leave out the times sign and write **3t**, which means "3 times t".

If a ticket costs $8, swap the t for 8: 3t = 3 × 8 = 24. The tickets cost $24 in total. Swapping a letter for a number like this is called **substituting**.

## Expressions and equations

An **expression** is a mix of numbers, letters and signs, like 2x + 5. It has no equals sign. You can work out its value if you know x, but there is nothing to solve.

An **equation** has an equals sign. It says that two things have the same value, like 2x + 5 = 17. Now there is a question to answer: what number must x be to make this true?

A quick way to tell them apart:

- 4a − 3 is an expression.
- 4a − 3 = 9 is an equation.

You can often tidy up an expression. In 3x + 2x + 7, the terms 3x and 2x are **like terms**, because they both have an x. Combine them: 3x + 2x = 5x. So the expression becomes 5x + 7. The 7 stays on its own, because it has no x to join with.

## Solving one-step equations

Think of an equation as a balance scale. Both sides weigh the same. If you do something to one side, you must do the same thing to the other side, or the scale tips over.

To solve an equation, you want x on its own. You get there by **undoing** whatever is being done to x. Adding is undone by subtracting, and multiplying is undone by dividing.

**Example 1:** x + 9 = 15

Nine is being added to x, so subtract 9 from both sides. That gives x = 15 − 9, so **x = 6**.

**Example 2:** 4x = 28

Here x is being multiplied by 4, so divide both sides by 4. That gives x = 28 ÷ 4, so **x = 7**.

Always check by putting your answer back in. For example 2: 4 × 7 = 28. It works.

## Solving two-step equations

Some equations need two moves. Undo the adding or subtracting first, then undo the multiplying or dividing. It's a bit like getting undressed: you put your socks on before your shoes, so you take your shoes off first.

**Example:** 3x + 5 = 20

1. Subtract 5 from both sides: 3x = 15.
2. Divide both sides by 3: **x = 5**.

Check: 3 × 5 + 5 = 15 + 5 = 20. Correct.

**Example with a fraction:** x/2 + 3 = 10

1. Subtract 3 from both sides: x/2 = 7.
2. Multiply both sides by 2: **x = 14**.

Check: 14 ÷ 2 + 3 = 7 + 3 = 10. Correct.

**A word problem:** A phone plan costs $10 a month plus $2 for every gigabyte of data. Last month's bill was $26. How many gigabytes were used?

Let g be the number of gigabytes. The bill is 2g + 10, so the equation is 2g + 10 = 26. Subtract 10 to get 2g = 16. Divide by 2 to get **g = 8**. So 8 gigabytes were used.

## What comes next

Once two-step equations feel easy, you'll start to meet a few new ideas.

**Letters on both sides**, like 5x + 2 = 3x + 10. First get all the x terms on one side. Take 3x from both sides to get 2x + 2 = 10. Then 2x = 8, so x = 4.

**Brackets**, like 2(x + 3) = 14. Multiply out the bracket first: 2x + 6 = 14. Then 2x = 8, so x = 4.

**Squares**, like x² = 9. An equation with x² in it is called a **quadratic**, and it can have two answers. Here x = 3 or x = −3, because 3 × 3 = 9 and (−3) × (−3) = 9 too.
