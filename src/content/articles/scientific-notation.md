Scientific notation is a short way of writing very large or very small numbers using powers of ten. Instead of counting long strings of zeros, you write one tidy expression.

For example, 4,500 becomes 4.5 × 10³, and 0.0032 becomes 3.2 × 10⁻³. Scientists use it for things like the distance to stars or the size of atoms.

## The format

A number in scientific notation has two parts:

- A number from 1 up to (but not including) 10. This is the **coefficient**.
- A power of ten that says how far the decimal point moves.

So it always looks like *a* × 10ⁿ, where *a* is at least 1 and less than 10. The exponent *n* is a whole number that can be positive or negative.

## Converting a large number

A positive exponent means the number is 10 or bigger. The exponent counts how many places the decimal point moves to the left.

### Worked example: write 4,500 in scientific notation

1. Place the decimal point after the first non-zero digit: 4.5
2. Count how many places the point moved from the end of 4,500 to there: 3 places.
3. The number is large, so the exponent is positive.

So 4,500 = **4.5 × 10³**. Check: 4.5 × 1,000 = 4,500.

## Converting a small number

A negative exponent means the number is less than 1. The exponent counts how many places the decimal point moves to the right.

### Worked example: write 0.0032 in scientific notation

1. Place the decimal point after the first non-zero digit: 3.2
2. Count how many places the point moved: from 0.0032 to 3.2 is 3 places.
3. The number is small, so the exponent is negative.

So 0.0032 = **3.2 × 10⁻³**. Check: 3.2 ÷ 1,000 = 0.0032.

## Multiplying in scientific notation

To multiply, multiply the coefficients and add the exponents.

### Worked example: (2 × 10³) × (3 × 10⁴)

1. Multiply the coefficients: 2 × 3 = 6.
2. Add the exponents: 3 + 4 = 7.

So the answer is **6 × 10⁷**, which is 60,000,000. Check: 2,000 × 30,000 = 60,000,000.

## Common mistakes

- **A coefficient that is not between 1 and 10.** 45 × 10² is not proper scientific notation. Rewrite it as 4.5 × 10³.
- **Counting the wrong direction.** Large numbers get a positive exponent; small numbers (less than 1) get a negative one.
- **Miscounting the places.** Line up your original number and the new decimal position, and count each jump carefully.
- **Adding exponents when you should not.** You only add exponents when multiplying. To multiply you add; to divide you subtract.

## Scientific notation vs standard form: don't mix them up

These two phrases sound alike but mean opposite things:

- **Scientific notation** is the short form: 4.5 × 10³.
- **Standard form** (in the UK; "standard notation" in the US) is the ordinary way: 4,500.

So when a question says "write 4.5 × 10³ in standard form", it is asking you to expand it back out: **4,500**. Read the question twice — many students convert the wrong way.

## Quick reference: powers of ten

| Power | Value | Example |
|-------|-------|---------|
| 10³ | 1,000 | 4.5 × 10³ = 4,500 |
| 10² | 100 | 7 × 10² = 700 |
| 10¹ | 10 | 9.1 × 10¹ = 91 |
| 10⁰ | 1 | anything × 10⁰ stays the same |
| 10⁻¹ | 0.1 | 3 × 10⁻¹ = 0.3 |
| 10⁻² | 0.01 | 3 × 10⁻² = 0.03 |
| 10⁻³ | 0.001 | 3.2 × 10⁻³ = 0.0032 |

## Worked example: going back the other way

### Example: write 6.8 × 10⁴ as an ordinary number

1. The exponent 4 is positive, so this is a big number: move the decimal point 4 places to the right.
2. 6.8 → 68 → 680 → 6,800 → 68,000.

So 6.8 × 10⁴ = **68,000**.

### Example: write 7.1 × 10⁻⁴ as an ordinary number

1. The exponent −4 is negative, so move the decimal point 4 places to the left.
2. 7.1 → 0.71 → 0.071 → 0.0071 → 0.00071.

So 7.1 × 10⁻⁴ = **0.00071**.

## Where you'll use it

- **Space.** The Sun is about 1.5 × 10⁸ km from Earth — far tidier than 150,000,000 km.
- **Tiny things.** A water molecule is about 2.75 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across.
- **Computers.** A terabyte is roughly 10¹² bytes.
- **Your calculator.** When a calculator shows 6.8E4, it means 6.8 × 10⁴.

## Two quick questions

**Can the exponent be zero?**
Yes. 10⁰ = 1, so 5.2 × 10⁰ is just 5.2. You will rarely see it, but it is valid.

**Is 0.45 × 10³ scientific notation?**
No — the coefficient 0.45 is less than 1. Fix it by moving the point: 0.45 × 10³ = 4.5 × 10². Whenever the coefficient is out of range, shift the point and adjust the exponent in the opposite direction.

**Why not just write all the zeros?**
You could, but long strings of zeros are where mistakes hide — it is easy to write one too many or too few. Scientific notation keeps the important digits (the coefficient) separate from the size (the power of ten), so each part can be checked on its own.

## Try it yourself

1. Write 68,000 in scientific notation.
2. Write 0.00071 in scientific notation.

**Answers:** 1) The coefficient is 6.8 and the point moves 4 places, so **6.8 × 10⁴**. 2) The coefficient is 7.1 and the point moves 4 places to the right, so **7.1 × 10⁻⁴**.
