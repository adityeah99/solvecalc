The distance from Earth to the Sun is 150,000,000 km. Go on, count those zeros. Did you get eight? Most people miscount. That's exactly the problem scientific notation solves.

It's a short way of writing very large or very small numbers using powers of ten. Instead of drowning in zeros, you write one tidy expression. 150,000,000 km becomes 1.5 × 10⁸. Much harder to miscount.

## The format

Every number in scientific notation has two parts:

- A number from 1 up to (but not including) 10. Call it the **coefficient**.
- A power of ten that says how far the decimal point moved.

So it always looks like *a* × 10ⁿ, where *a* sits between 1 and 10, and *n* is a whole number that can be positive or negative.

## Converting a large number

A positive exponent means the number is 10 or bigger. The exponent counts how many places the decimal point moved to the left.

### Worked example: write 4,500 in scientific notation

1. Put the decimal point after the first non-zero digit: 4.5
2. Count how far the point moved from the end of 4,500 to get there: 3 places.
3. The number is large, so the exponent is positive.

So 4,500 = **4.5 × 10³**. Check: 4.5 × 1,000 = 4,500. It works.

## Converting a small number

A negative exponent means the number is less than 1. Same counting, opposite direction: the exponent counts how many places the point moved to the right.

### Worked example: write 0.0032 in scientific notation

1. Put the decimal point after the first non-zero digit: 3.2
2. Count the move: from 0.0032 to 3.2 is 3 places.
3. The number is small, so the exponent is negative.

So 0.0032 = **3.2 × 10⁻³**. Check: 3.2 ÷ 1,000 = 0.0032.

## Multiplying in scientific notation

Here's the payoff. To multiply, just multiply the coefficients and add the exponents. No zero-counting at all.

### Worked example: (2 × 10³) × (3 × 10⁴)

1. Multiply the coefficients: 2 × 3 = 6.
2. Add the exponents: 3 + 4 = 7.

So the answer is **6 × 10⁷**, which is 60,000,000. Check the long way: 2,000 × 30,000 = 60,000,000. Same answer, far less squinting.

## The mistakes I see every year

- **A coefficient outside 1 to 10.** 45 × 10² is not proper scientific notation. Slide the point: 4.5 × 10³.
- **The exponent pointing the wrong way.** Big numbers get positive exponents. Numbers smaller than 1 get negative ones. Say it as a rule and you'll stop mixing them up.
- **Miscounting the jumps.** Line up your original number and the new decimal position, and count each jump one by one. Rushing this step is where most errors live.
- **Adding exponents when you shouldn't.** You only add exponents when multiplying. Multiplying: add. Dividing: subtract. That's the whole rule.

## Scientific notation vs standard form: read the question twice

These two phrases sound alike but mean opposite things, and this catches my students constantly:

- **Scientific notation** is the short form: 4.5 × 10³.
- **Standard form** (in the UK; "standard notation" in the US) is the ordinary way: 4,500.

So when a question says "write 4.5 × 10³ in standard form", it wants you to expand it back out: **4,500**. Read the question twice. Half the class converts the wrong way on the first try. I used to do it too.

## Quick reference: powers of ten

| Power | Value | Example |
|-------|-------|---------|
| 10³ | 1,000 | 4.5 × 10³ = 4,500 |
| 10² | 100 | 7 × 10² = 700 |
| 10¹ | 10 | 9.1 × 10¹ = 91 |
| 10⁰ | 1 | anything × 10⁰ stays the same |
| 10⁻¹ | 0.1 | 3 × 10⁻¹ = 0.3 |
| 10⁻² | 0.01 | 3 × 10⁻² = 0.03 |
| 10⁻³ | 0.001 | 3.2 × 10⁻³ = 0.0032 |

## Worked example: going back the other way

### Example: write 6.8 × 10⁴ as an ordinary number

1. The exponent 4 is positive, so this is a big number: move the decimal point 4 places to the right.
2. 6.8 → 68 → 680 → 6,800 → 68,000.

So 6.8 × 10⁴ = **68,000**.

### Example: write 7.1 × 10⁻⁴ as an ordinary number

1. The exponent −4 is negative, so move the decimal point 4 places to the left.
2. 7.1 → 0.71 → 0.071 → 0.0071 → 0.00071.

So 7.1 × 10⁻⁴ = **0.00071**.

## Where you'll actually meet it

- **Space.** The Sun is about 1.5 × 10⁸ km away. Far tidier than 150,000,000 km, and you can't miscount.
- **Tiny things.** A water molecule is about 2.75 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across. Try writing that one out longhand.
- **Computers.** A terabyte is roughly 10¹² bytes.
- **Your calculator.** When it shows 6.8E4, that's just its way of writing 6.8 × 10⁴. Now you know what it's telling you.

## Two quick questions

**Can the exponent be zero?**
Yes. 10⁰ = 1, so 5.2 × 10⁰ is just 5.2. You'll rarely see it, but it's valid.

**Is 0.45 × 10³ scientific notation?**
No. The coefficient 0.45 is less than 1. Fix it by shifting the point: 0.45 × 10³ = 4.5 × 10². Whenever the coefficient is out of range, slide the point and adjust the exponent the opposite way.

**Why not just write all the zeros?**
You could. But long strings of zeros are where mistakes hide. It's embarrassingly easy to write one too many or one too few. Scientific notation splits the job: the coefficient holds the important digits, the power of ten holds the size. Each part gets checked on its own.

## Try it yourself

1. Write 68,000 in scientific notation.
2. Write 0.00071 in scientific notation.

**Answers:** 1) The coefficient is 6.8 and the point moves 4 places, so **6.8 × 10⁴**. 2) The coefficient is 7.1 and the point moves 4 places to the right, so **7.1 × 10⁻⁴**.
