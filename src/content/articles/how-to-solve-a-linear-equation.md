Solving an equation means finding the value of the letter that makes both sides equal. For 3x − 5 = 16, you're hunting for the number x that makes the left side come out to 16.

The entire method hangs on one idea, and I want you to really get this one: an equation is a balance scale. Do the same thing to both sides, and it stays balanced. Tip it on one side only, and everything falls apart.

## The balance rule

You're allowed to:

- add the same number to both sides
- subtract the same number from both sides
- multiply both sides by the same number
- divide both sides by the same number (as long as it isn't zero)

Your goal: get the letter alone on one side. To do that, undo whatever's being done to it, working backwards.

## Example 1: a one-step equation

Solve x + 7 = 15.

1. Something's being added to x: the 7. Undo it by subtracting 7 from both sides.
2. x + 7 − 7 = 15 − 7
3. x = 8

**Check:** 8 + 7 = 15. Correct.

Short, sweet, and it teaches you the most important habit in algebra: always check by plugging your answer back in. Takes five seconds. Saves you constantly.

## Example 2: a two-step equation

Solve 3x − 5 = 16.

Here x gets multiplied by 3, then 5 is taken away. Undo in reverse order: deal with the − 5 first, then the × 3.

1. Add 5 to both sides: 3x = 21
2. Divide both sides by 3: x = 7

**Check:** 3 × 7 − 5 = 21 − 5 = 16. Correct.

Reverse order matters. If you divided by 3 first, you'd get x − 5/3 = 16/3, which is technically fine but now you're swimming in fractions for no reason. Undo the last thing done first, like taking off your shoes before your socks.

## Example 3: letters on both sides

Solve 5x + 2 = 2x + 14.

When x shows up on both sides, gather all the x terms onto one side first.

1. Subtract 2x from both sides: 3x + 2 = 14
2. Subtract 2 from both sides: 3x = 12
3. Divide both sides by 3: x = 4

**Check:** Left side is 5 × 4 + 2 = 22. Right side is 2 × 4 + 14 = 22. Both match.

My advice: move the smaller x term, so you're left with a positive number of x's. Fewer negatives, fewer sign mistakes.

## Example 4: an equation with brackets

Solve 4(x − 3) = 20.

1. Multiply out the bracket. The 4 hits both parts: 4x − 12 = 20
2. Add 12 to both sides: 4x = 32
3. Divide both sides by 4: x = 8

**Check:** 4 × (8 − 3) = 4 × 5 = 20. Correct.

There's a shortcut hiding here too. Since everything on the left is multiplied by 4, you could divide both sides by 4 first: x − 3 = 5, so x = 8. Two steps instead of three. Shortcuts like this appear once you're comfortable; don't force them early.

## Example 5: an equation with a fraction

Solve x/3 + 2 = 6.

1. Subtract 2 from both sides: x/3 = 4
2. x is being divided by 3, so multiply both sides by 3: x = 12

**Check:** 12 ÷ 3 + 2 = 4 + 2 = 6. Correct.

## Putting it to work: a real problem

Three friends go to the movies. They buy three tickets and share one popcorn that costs $6. The total is $36. How much is one ticket?

1. Let t be the price of one ticket. Three tickets plus popcorn is 3t + 6.
2. Write the equation: 3t + 6 = 36
3. Subtract 6 from both sides: 3t = 30
4. Divide by 3: t = 10
5. Each ticket costs $10.

This is the part students skip, and it costs them marks: answer the original question in words at the end. "t = 10" is math; "each ticket costs $10" is an answer.

## Don't panic at negative answers

Solve −2x = 10. Divide both sides by −2 and you get x = −5. Check: −2 × −5 = 10. Negative times negative is positive, so it works fine.

A negative answer isn't a sign you did something wrong. It's just an answer that happens to be negative.

## Mistakes I see every year

- **Doing something to only one side.** Subtract 7 on the left, you subtract 7 on the right too. The scale doesn't negotiate.
- **Sign slips.** Moving a term across flips + to − and vice versa. Write the step out in full ("subtract 2x from both sides") instead of jumping ahead in your head.
- **Only multiplying the first part of a bracket.** 4(x − 3) is 4x − 12, not 4x − 3. The 4 multiplies everything inside. Everything.
- **Skipping the check.** Plugging your answer back into the original equation takes seconds and catches most mistakes. I genuinely can't stress this enough.

## Expressions vs equations: stop mixing these up

An **expression** like 3x + 5 has no equals sign. It's a recipe, not a question. You can simplify an expression (2x + 3x becomes 5x), but you can't "solve" it, because there's nothing to balance. Asking "what is x in 3x + 5?" has no answer. x could be anything.

An **equation** like 3x + 5 = 20 has two sides joined by an equals sign. Now there's something to balance, and exactly one value of x makes it true.

This distinction trips up so many students that I make a point of it every term: if you catch yourself trying to solve something with no equals sign, stop. You're holding an expression. Either simplify it, or check whether part of the equation got left behind.

## Example 6: an equation with decimals

Solve 0.5x + 3 = 9.

Decimals behave exactly like whole numbers. Undo in reverse order.

1. Subtract 3 from both sides: 0.5x = 6
2. Divide both sides by 0.5: x = 12

**Check:** 0.5 × 12 + 3 = 6 + 3 = 9. Correct.

Handy trick: multiply the whole equation by 10 to clear decimals first. 0.5x + 3 = 9 becomes 5x + 30 = 90, and x = 12 falls out quickly from there.

## Example 7: an age puzzle

Mia is 4 years older than her brother. Together, their ages add up to 28. How old is each of them?

1. Let b stand for the brother's age. Then Mia's age is b + 4.
2. Write the equation: b + (b + 4) = 28, which simplifies to 2b + 4 = 28
3. Subtract 4 from both sides: 2b = 24
4. Divide by 2: b = 12
5. The brother is 12, and Mia is 12 + 4 = 16.

**Check:** 12 + 16 = 28, and 16 is 4 more than 12. Both conditions hold.

See the pattern? Name the unknown, translate the words into an equation, solve it, then answer the original question in words. That four-step rhythm handles almost every word problem you'll meet.

## Quick questions

**What if the x just... disappears?**

Then the equation is telling you something about itself. For 2x + 3 = 2x + 7, subtracting 2x from both sides leaves 3 = 7, which is false. No value of x works: there is **no solution**. For 2x + 3 = 2x + 3, you get 3 = 3, always true: **every** number works. Both are perfectly valid answers, and students are always surprised by that.

**Can I start from the right side?**

Yes. The balance rule works in either direction. For 20 = 3x + 5, subtract 5 from both sides to get 15 = 3x, then divide by 3: 5 = x, so x = 5. Some people find it easier to flip the equation around first and write 3x + 5 = 20.

**Do I really have to write every step?**

While you're learning, yes. Every written step is a chance to catch a sign slip before it wrecks the answer. Once the moves feel automatic you can combine steps, but keep the check at the end. Always keep the check.

## Try it yourself

1. Solve 6x + 4 = 40.
2. Solve 2(x + 5) = 3x + 1.

**Answers:** 1) 6x = 36, so x = 6. 2) 2x + 10 = 3x + 1. Subtract 2x: 10 = x + 1, so x = 9. Check: 2 × 14 = 28 and 3 × 9 + 1 = 28.
