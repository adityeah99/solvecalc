Half a sandwich. 0.5 of a sandwich. Same amount, two costumes.

Fractions and decimals are just two ways of writing the same amount, and switching between them is a skill you'll use constantly: in shops, in recipes, in test scores. The whole trick fits in one sentence: **a fraction is a division**. The line in 3/4 means "divided by". So 3/4 is 3 ÷ 4, which is 0.75. That's really all there is to it.

## Method 1: divide the top by the bottom

This method always works, no exceptions. Divide the numerator (top number) by the denominator (bottom number).

### Example: 3/4

1. Write it as a division: 3 ÷ 4
2. 4 does not go into 3, so write 0 and a decimal point, then think of 3 as 3.0
3. 4 goes into 30 seven times (7 × 4 = 28), with 2 left over.
4. Bring down another 0 to make 20. 4 goes into 20 five times, with nothing left.
5. So 3/4 = 0.75.

### Example: 3/8

1. Write it as a division: 3 ÷ 8
2. 8 goes into 30 three times (3 × 8 = 24), with 6 left over.
3. Bring down a 0 to make 60. 8 goes into 60 seven times (56), with 4 left over.
4. Bring down a 0 to make 40. 8 goes into 40 five times, with nothing left.
5. So 3/8 = 0.375.

On a calculator you'd just type `3/8`. But knowing the hand method means you understand what the calculator is doing.

## Method 2: make the bottom 10, 100 or 1000

If the denominator can easily become 10, 100 or 1000, you can skip the long division entirely.

### Example: 7/20

1. What do you multiply 20 by to get 100? The answer is 5.
2. Multiply the top and bottom by 5: 7/20 = 35/100
3. 35 hundredths is written as 0.35.

This works beautifully for denominators like 2, 4, 5, 20, 25 and 50. For example, 9/25 = 36/100 = 0.36. I always check for this shortcut before reaching for long division. It saves real time.

## Decimals that repeat (and why)

Some fractions never finish when you divide. Try 1/3:

- 3 goes into 10 three times, with 1 left over.
- Bring down a 0 to make 10 again. 3 goes in three times, with 1 left over.
- This happens again and again forever.

So 1/3 = 0.333… where the 3 keeps going. This is called a **repeating decimal**, often written with a dot or a bar over the repeating digit.

A few more you'll meet:

- 2/3 = 0.666…
- 5/6 = 0.8333… (only the 3 repeats)
- 2/11 = 0.1818… (the pair "18" repeats)

### How to tell if a decimal will stop

First simplify the fraction. Then look at the denominator's prime factors. If the only primes dividing it are 2 and 5, the decimal stops. If any other prime divides it, like 3, 7 or 11, the decimal repeats.

- 3/8 stops, because 8 = 2 × 2 × 2.
- 7/20 stops, because 20 = 2 × 2 × 5.
- 5/6 repeats, because 6 = 2 × 3, and 3 is not 2 or 5.

This is one of my favorite little rules. It feels like a magic trick the first time you use it.

## Mixed numbers

For a mixed number like 2 3/5, keep the whole number and convert only the fraction part.

1. Convert 3/5: 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6
2. Add the whole number: 2 + 0.6 = 2.6

## Common fractions worth memorizing

| Fraction | Decimal | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| 1/2 | 0.5 | 50% |
| 1/3 | 0.333… | 33.3…% |
| 1/4 | 0.25 | 25% |
| 3/4 | 0.75 | 75% |
| 1/5 | 0.2 | 20% |
| 1/8 | 0.125 | 12.5% |
| 1/10 | 0.1 | 10% |

Honestly, just memorize this table. It'll pay you back hundreds of times. To turn a decimal into a percentage, multiply by 100, so 0.375 becomes 37.5%. There's more on this in [how to calculate a percentage](/articles/how-to-calculate-a-percentage).

## The mistakes I keep seeing

- **Dividing the wrong way round.** 3/4 means 3 ÷ 4, not 4 ÷ 3. The top number goes first. Every year someone gets this backwards on a test.
- **Reading the digits as a decimal.** 1/4 is not 1.4 or 0.14. It's 0.25.
- **Cutting a repeating decimal too short without saying so.** 1/3 is not exactly 0.33. If you round, use ≈ and say how many places, like 1/3 ≈ 0.33.
- **Changing only the bottom.** In Method 2, multiply the top and bottom by the same number. Both. Always.

### Example: 7/40

1. Write it as a division: 7 ÷ 40.
2. 40 goes into 70 once (1 × 40 = 40), with 30 left over.
3. Bring down a 0 to make 300. 40 goes into 300 seven times (280), with 20 left over.
4. Bring down a 0 to make 200. 40 goes into 200 five times, with nothing left.
5. So 7/40 = 0.175. It stops, because 40 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 5.

## Going the other way: decimal to fraction

Converting works in both directions. To turn a decimal into a fraction:

1. Write the decimal over 1: 0.6 becomes 6/10.
2. Move the decimal point right until the top is a whole number: 6/10 (one place for one digit).
3. Simplify: 6/10 = 3/5.

More digits need bigger bottoms:

- 0.25 = 25/100 = 1/4
- 0.125 = 125/1000 = 1/8
- 1.5 = 15/10 = 3/2, or 1 1/2 as a mixed number

See the pattern? The number of decimal digits tells you the bottom. One digit means tenths, two digits means hundredths, three digits means thousandths.

## Where you'll actually use this

- **Money.** Prices are decimals: $3.75 is 3 3/4 dollars. Coins are fractions of a dollar. A quarter is literally called a quarter because it's 1/4.
- **Recipes.** A recipe calling for 0.5 cups of milk means 1/2 cup, and 0.25 cups is 1/4 cup. Thinking in fractions makes measuring cups easier.
- **Sports.** A basketball player who makes 7 of 10 free throws shoots 0.7. You'd say "seven out of ten", which is the fraction 7/10.
- **Grades.** A test score of 18/20 is the same as 0.9, which is 90%. Fractions, decimals and percentages travel together everywhere.

## Mini FAQs

**Is 0.999… really equal to 1?**
Yes, and it bothers everyone the first time. A student asked me this last month, completely sure it had to be "almost 1 but not quite". Here's the proof: 1/3 = 0.333… and 2/3 = 0.666…, so three thirds is 0.999…. But three thirds is also 1. So 0.999… = 1. It's a quirk of how repeating decimals work, and it's genuinely true.

**What is 1/7 as a decimal?**
It's 0.142857142857…, with the six digits "142857" repeating forever. Long repeating patterns like this are normal. Every fraction either stops or repeats, no exceptions.

**Should I use a decimal or a fraction in my answer?**
Fractions are exact: 1/3 is exactly one third. Decimals are often rounded: 0.33 is only close. In school, prefer the fraction unless the question asks for a decimal.
