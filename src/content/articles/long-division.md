Long division is how you divide big numbers by hand, one digit at a time. It works for any division, even when the numbers are way too big to do in your head.

The whole thing is a short cycle you repeat: divide, multiply, subtract, bring down. Four moves. Once that rhythm clicks, long division turns into something you can do almost on autopilot. I promise it looks scarier than it is.

## The words you need

Quick vocabulary, because teachers love throwing these around:

- **Dividend:** the number being divided (goes inside).
- **Divisor:** the number you divide by (goes outside).
- **Quotient:** the answer (goes on top).
- **Remainder:** what's left over when the divisor doesn't fit exactly.

Mix up dividend and divisor and you'll divide everything backwards, so get these straight early.

## The four-step cycle

For each digit, repeat:

1. **Divide:** how many times does the divisor fit?
2. **Multiply:** multiply that answer by the divisor.
3. **Subtract:** take it away to see what's left.
4. **Bring down:** carry down the next digit and start again.

Divide, multiply, subtract, bring down. Say it in your head like a chant while you work. My students who chant it make fewer mistakes. I'm not joking.

## Worked example: 512 ÷ 16

Divisor 16, dividend 512.

1. Does 16 go into 5? No, 16 is bigger than 5. So start with the first two digits: 51.
2. Does 16 go into 51? Yes, 3 times, because 3 × 16 = 48. Write 3 in the quotient. Subtract: 51 − 48 = 3.
3. Bring down the next digit, 2, to make 32.
4. Does 16 go into 32? Yes, exactly 2 times, because 2 × 16 = 32. Write 2. Subtract: 32 − 32 = 0.

Nothing left. The answer is **32**. Check by multiplying back: 32 × 16 = 512. That check is non-negotiable while you're learning.

## Worked example with a remainder: 953 ÷ 4

Divisor 4, dividend 953.

1. Does 4 go into 9? Yes, 2 times, because 2 × 4 = 8. Write 2. Subtract: 9 − 8 = 1.
2. Bring down the 5 to make 15. Does 4 go into 15? Yes, 3 times, because 3 × 4 = 12. Write 3. Subtract: 15 − 12 = 3.
3. Bring down the 3 to make 33. Does 4 go into 33? Yes, 8 times, because 8 × 4 = 32. Write 8. Subtract: 33 − 32 = 1.
4. No more digits to bring down, and 1 is left over.

So 953 ÷ 4 = **238 remainder 1**. Check: 238 × 4 = 952, and 952 + 1 = 953.

## Where everyone goes wrong

- **Skipping the "bring down" step.** After every subtraction, pull down the next digit before dividing again. Forget it once and the whole answer shifts.
- **Misaligning the columns.** Keep each quotient digit above the digit you're working on. Let the columns drift and your place values drift with them.
- **Picking a quotient digit that's too big or too small.** If your subtraction gives a number as big as the divisor, your digit was too small. If the multiply overshoots the working number, it was too big. Adjust and carry on.
- **Forgetting the remainder.** Clean division, great. Leftover? Write it down as the remainder. Don't just drop it.

## Long division vs short division

**Short division** is the compact version for single-digit divisors, when you can hold the remainders in your head. For 864 ÷ 8, you work left to right: 8 into 8 is 1, 8 into 6 is 0 with 6 carried, making 64, and 8 into 64 is 8. Answer: 108. Fast, but only while the mental juggling stays easy.

**Long division** writes every step down, which is why it handles bigger divisors like 16 or 27 without breaking a sweat. The trade-off is space: it takes more room on the page, but it never asks you to hold more than one small step in your head.

My rule of thumb: short division for single-digit divisors you're comfortable with, long division for everything else. Same mathematics, just different amounts of working shown.

## Worked example with a decimal answer: 875 ÷ 4

Sometimes you don't want a remainder. You want the decimal.

1. Does 4 go into 8? Yes, 2 times, because 2 × 4 = 8. Write 2. Subtract: 8 − 8 = 0.
2. Bring down the 7. Does 4 go into 7? Yes, 1 time, because 1 × 4 = 4. Write 1. Subtract: 7 − 4 = 3.
3. Bring down the 5 to make 35. Does 4 go into 35? Yes, 8 times, because 8 × 4 = 32. Write 8. Subtract: 35 − 32 = 3.
4. No more digits, but 3 is left. Write a decimal point in the quotient and add a 0 to the dividend, making 30.
5. Does 4 go into 30? Yes, 7 times, because 7 × 4 = 28. Write 7 after the point. Subtract: 30 − 28 = 2.
6. Add another 0, making 20. Does 4 go into 20? Yes, exactly 5 times. Write 5. Subtract: 20 − 20 = 0.

So 875 ÷ 4 = **218.75**. Check: 218.75 × 4 = 875.

The decimal-point trick is just "keep bringing down zeros". Once you see that, remainders and decimals are the same process.

## The cycle, on one card

| Step | What to do | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Divide | See how many times the divisor fits | "Does it fit? How many times?" |
| 2. Multiply | Multiply that digit by the divisor | "What's 3 × 16?" |
| 3. Subtract | Take it away from the working number | "What's left?" |
| 4. Bring down | Carry down the next digit | "Any digits left?" |

Repeat until the digits run out. Leftover at the end? That's the remainder. Or keep going past a decimal point for the decimal answer.

## Where you'll actually use this

- **Splitting costs.** Four friends share an $875 holiday cabin. 875 ÷ 4 = 218.75, so each pays $218.75. Somebody always does this math on their phone at the table.
- **Time.** A 953-minute playlist is 953 ÷ 60 hours. That's 15 hours with 53 minutes left over. Long division handles the remainder naturally.
- **Batches.** 512 stickers, packs hold 16. 512 ÷ 16 = 32 packs exactly, no leftovers. Satisfying.

## Quick questions

**What if the divisor is bigger than the first digit?**

Start with the first two digits, like the 512 ÷ 16 example did with 51. If the divisor beats the first two digits as well (happens with three-digit divisors), start with the first three. Keep widening until the divisor fits.

**Should I check my answer on a calculator?**

Yes, while you're learning. Divide on the calculator and compare. If it shows a decimal like 238.25 and your hand working gave 238 remainder 1, check: 0.25 × 4 = 1, so they match. The calculator is your sparring partner, not your enemy.

## Try it yourself

1. Work out 864 ÷ 8.
2. Work out 275 ÷ 6.

**Answers:** 1) 8 into 86 goes 10 times (80), leaving 6; bring down the 4 to make 64; 8 into 64 goes 8 times, so the answer is **108**. Check: 108 × 8 = 864. 2) 6 into 27 is 4 (24), leaving 3; bring down the 5 to make 35; 6 into 35 is 5 (30), leaving 5, so the answer is **45 remainder 5**. Check: 45 × 6 = 270, plus 5 is 275.
