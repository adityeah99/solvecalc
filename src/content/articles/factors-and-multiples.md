Quick question before we start: what's the difference between a factor and a multiple?

If you hesitated, you're in good company. My students mix these two words up more than any other pair in arithmetic. They sound similar, they show up together, and your brain files them in the same drawer.

Here's the drawer, properly organized. Factors and multiples are two sides of one idea: a factor divides a number exactly, while a multiple is what you get when you multiply a number. Nail the difference now and a lot of later math gets easier.

## What is a factor?

A factor of a number divides into it with no remainder. To list the factors, hunt for pairs that multiply to give the number.

### Worked example: factors of 24

Find pairs that multiply to 24:

- 1 × 24
- 2 × 12
- 3 × 8
- 4 × 6

So the factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24. Every number has 1 and itself as factors. Those two are freebies, always include them.

## What is a multiple?

A multiple of a number is that number times 1, 2, 3, and so on. The multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and they carry on forever.

There's no largest multiple, so the list never ends. And here's a neat link between the two ideas: a number is always a multiple of each of its factors.

## Greatest common factor (GCF)

The greatest common factor of two numbers is the largest number that divides both of them. Its main job in life is simplifying fractions.

### Worked example: GCF of 24 and 36

1. Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.
2. Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.
3. Factors in both lists: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.
4. The largest shared factor is 12.

So the GCF of 24 and 36 is **12**.

## Lowest common multiple (LCM)

The lowest common multiple of two numbers is the smallest number that both of them divide into. Its main job is adding fractions with different denominators.

### Worked example: LCM of 4 and 6

1. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24.
2. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30.
3. The first number in both lists is 12.

So the LCM of 4 and 6 is **12**. Notice that 12 is a multiple of both numbers, which is exactly what "common multiple" means.

## The mix-ups I see constantly

- **Swapping the words.** Factors go *into* a number; multiples come *out* of it. Factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Multiples of 12 are 12, 24, 36, and on forever.
- **Missing factor pairs.** Work up from 1 in order so you don't skip a pair. It's embarrassingly easy to forget 4 × 6 when listing the factors of 24. I still double-check myself.
- **Mixing up GCF and LCM.** The greatest common *factor* is small (it divides the numbers). The lowest common *multiple* is larger (the numbers divide into it).
- **Forgetting 1 and the number itself.** Both are always factors. Always.

## Quick reference table

| Question | What you want | How to find it | Bigger or smaller than the numbers? |
|---|---|---|---|
| What are the factors of 12? | 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 | Pairs that multiply to 12 | Smaller than or equal to 12 |
| What are the multiples of 12? | 12, 24, 36, … | 12 × 1, 12 × 2, 12 × 3, … | Bigger than or equal to 12 |
| What is the GCF of 8 and 12? | 4 | Biggest number dividing both | Smaller than both |
| What is the LCM of 8 and 12? | 24 | Smallest number both divide into | Bigger than both |

If you ever blank on which is which in a test, remember this: **factors are small, multiples are big.** GCF is the smaller idea, LCM is the bigger one.

## Extra worked example: GCF of 48 and 18

Listing works fine for bigger numbers too, as long as you're systematic about it.

1. Factors of 48. Work up in pairs: 1 × 48, 2 × 24, 3 × 16, 4 × 12, 6 × 8. So the factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48.
2. Factors of 18. Pairs: 1 × 18, 2 × 9, 3 × 6. So the factors are 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18.
3. Factors in both lists: 1, 2, 3, 6.
4. The largest is 6.

So the GCF of 48 and 18 is **6**. Check: 48 ÷ 6 = 8 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3, both exact.

## Extra worked example: LCM of 9 and 12

1. Multiples of 9: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45.
2. Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48.
3. The first number in both lists is 36.

So the LCM of 9 and 12 is **36**. Check: 36 ÷ 9 = 4 and 36 ÷ 12 = 3, both exact.

A shortcut when the numbers share no factors: just multiply them. 9 and 12 share the factor 3, so that shortcut would give 108. Correct, but not the lowest. Listing finds the smallest one.

## Where you'll actually use this

- **Simplifying fractions.** The fraction 12/18 simplifies using the GCF of 12 and 18, which is 6: divide top and bottom by 6 to get 2/3. See [how to simplify fractions](/articles/how-to-simplify-fractions).
- **Sharing fairly.** You have 24 pencils and 36 stickers to pack into identical gift bags with nothing left over. The GCF of 24 and 36 is 12, so you can make 12 bags. Each gets 2 pencils and 3 stickers. Party planning is secretly a math test.
- **Tiling a floor.** Square tiles 30 cm across cover a 120 cm by 90 cm floor exactly: 120 and 90 are both multiples of 30, so the tiles fit with no cutting.
- **Syncing schedules.** One bus comes every 6 minutes, another every 8 minutes, and they leave together at 9:00. The LCM of 6 and 8 is 24, so they next leave together at 9:24.

## Mini FAQs

**Is 1 a factor of every number?**
Yes. Every number divides by 1 exactly, so 1 always appears in a factor list.

**Is 0 a multiple of every number?**
Yes, in a technical sense: any number times 0 is 0. In school problems the list usually starts at the number itself (6, 12, 18, …), so you can ignore 0 unless you're asked about it.

**What is the GCF of two prime numbers?**
It's always 1. Primes like 7 and 11 share no factors except 1. That's also why a fraction like 7/11 is already in its simplest form. There's nothing to cancel.
