Picture this: you're making pancakes for two friends, and the recipe calls for 2 cups of flour and 3 cups of milk. Then four more people show up. Now what? You don't guess. You scale. And scaling is exactly what ratios and proportions are for.

A ratio compares two amounts, side by side. A proportion says two ratios are equal, which is what lets you scale things up or down without breaking them.

## Writing and reading a ratio

You've got three ways to write "2 to 3": with a colon (2:3), in words (2 to 3), or as a fraction (2/3). Pick whichever the question uses.

One thing I drill into my students every year: the order matters. 2:3 is flour to milk. 3:2 is milk to flour. Flip them and your pancakes are a disaster. Keep the terms in the order the question gives you.

## Simplifying a ratio

Same idea as simplifying a fraction. Divide both terms by their greatest common factor and you're done.

### Worked example: simplify 12:18

1. The greatest common factor of 12 and 18 is 6.
2. Divide both terms by 6: 12 ÷ 6 = 2 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3.

So 12:18 simplifies to **2:3**. Same comparison, smaller numbers. The way I remember it: you're not changing the relationship, you're just writing it in its tidiest clothes.

## Solving a proportion

A proportion is just two ratios claiming to be equal, like 3/4 = x/20. The move here is cross-multiplication. Multiply each top by the opposite bottom, set them equal, solve.

### Worked example: solve 3/4 = x/20

1. Cross-multiply: 3 × 20 = 4 × x, so 60 = 4x.
2. Divide both sides by 4: x = 60 ÷ 4 = 15.

So x = **15**. Always check: 15/20 simplifies to 3/4. The ratios match, so you're good.

## A real one: scaling that pancake recipe

Back to the kitchen. The recipe uses flour and milk in the ratio 2:3, and you've poured 9 cups of milk. How much flour?

1. Set it up flour over milk: 2/3 = x/9.
2. Cross-multiply: 2 × 9 = 3 × x, so 18 = 3x.
3. Divide by 3: x = 18 ÷ 3 = 6.

**6 cups of flour.** Check it: 6:9 simplifies to 2:3. Pancakes saved.

## Why everyone messes this up

I see the same four mistakes every single year:

- **Swapping the order.** 2:3 is not 3:2. Read the question, keep the order.
- **Simplifying only one term.** You have to divide BOTH parts by the same number. 12:18 does not become 2:18. Ever.
- **Cross-multiplying the wrong pairs.** Top times the OPPOSITE bottom. Not a top times its own bottom.
- **Dropping the units.** Cups to cups means your answer is in cups. Keep track.

## Ratio vs fraction: the difference that matters

They look alike. They answer different questions, and this is the part my students always trip on.

A fraction like 2/3 is about **one part out of a whole**. Two out of three pizza slices eaten? Fraction eaten: 2/3.

A ratio like 2:3 compares **one amount against another**, side by side. It doesn't have to add up to a whole at all.

But here's the link between them. If 2:3 compares parts of ONE whole, the total is 2 + 3 = 5 parts, so the first part is 2/5 of the whole. A class with a boys-to-girls ratio of 2:3 has 2/5 boys and 3/5 girls.

My memory aid for students: a fraction is "part of a whole", a ratio is "part compared to part". Say it once and it sticks.

## Unit rates: the "best buy" trick

A **unit rate** is a ratio squashed down to "per one". And you already use it. Every time you compare prices at the shop, that's a unit rate in disguise.

### Worked example: which bag of rice is cheaper?

- A 2 kg bag costs $5.
- A 5 kg bag costs $11.

1. Price per kg for the small bag: $5 ÷ 2 = $2.50 per kg.
2. Price per kg for the big bag: $11 ÷ 5 = $2.20 per kg.
3. $2.20 beats $2.50.

The big bag wins, even though it costs more overall. Same cross-multiplication idea, just wearing a shopping disguise. I tell my students: supermarkets are basically ratio exams with fluorescent lighting.

## Where ratios actually show up

- **Cooking.** Scaling that 4-person recipe up to 10 people? Proportion problem.
- **Maps.** A 1:50,000 scale means 1 cm on the map is 50,000 cm (500 m) out there in the world.
- **Money.** Exchange rates are ratios. 1 USD to 0.92 EUR, for example.
- **Medicine.** 5 mg per 1 kg of body weight is a ratio, and this one has to be scaled exactly. No guessing.

If you remember one line, make it this: a ratio compares two things, a proportion says two ratios are equal.

## Two quick questions

**Can a ratio use more than two numbers?**
Yes. A paint mix of 1:2:3 means 1 part red, 2 parts blue, 3 parts white. Total is 6 parts, so red is 1/6 of the mix.

**Do ratios keep their units?**
Sometimes. 2 cups to 3 cups is plain, the units cancel. But 60 miles to 1 hour (60 mph) keeps its units, because that's a rate.

## Try it yourself

1. Simplify the ratio 20:35.
2. Solve the proportion 2/5 = x/20.

**Answers:** 1) The greatest common factor of 20 and 35 is 5, so 20 ÷ 5 = 4 and 35 ÷ 5 = 7, giving **4:7**. 2) Cross-multiply: 2 × 20 = 5 × x, so 40 = 5x and x = **8**.
