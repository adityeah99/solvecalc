Picture this: you're splitting a dinner bill and someone says "just add 15%". Everyone nods like they know what that means. Half the table is guessing.

Percentages are everywhere: sale tags, test scores, phone batteries, weather apps. And here's what I tell my students on day one: almost every percentage question is one of three types. Learn the three types and you're done. No more nodding along.

## What "percent" actually means

The word "percent" comes from "per hundred". So 40% is the same as 40 out of 100, which is the fraction 40/100 or the decimal 0.40.

To turn a percentage into a decimal, divide it by 100. In practice that just means sliding the decimal point two places left:

- 25% = 0.25
- 7% = 0.07
- 150% = 1.5

This decimal form is the one you'll calculate with. Get comfortable with the slide.

## Type 1: finding a percentage of a number

The most common question of all. "What is 15% of 200?"

The method: change the percentage to a decimal, then multiply.

### Example: 15% of 200

1. Change 15% to a decimal: 15 ÷ 100 = 0.15
2. Multiply: 0.15 × 200 = 30
3. So 15% of 200 is 30.

On a calculator you could type `200*15%` or `0.15*200`.

### Example: a phone on sale

A phone costs $80 and the shop takes 25% off. How much do you pay?

1. Change 25% to a decimal: 0.25
2. Find the discount: 0.25 × 80 = 20. The discount is $20.
3. Subtract from the price: 80 − 20 = 60. You pay $60.

A quicker way: if 25% is taken off, you pay the other 75%. So 0.75 × 80 = 60, same answer in one step. I love this shortcut. Use it.

## Type 2: what percent is one number of another?

Now you know the part and the whole, and you want the percentage. The method: divide the part by the whole, then multiply by 100.

### Example: a test score

You got 18 out of 24 on a science test. What is your score as a percentage?

1. Divide the part by the whole: 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75
2. Multiply by 100: 0.75 × 100 = 75
3. Your score is 75%.

The "whole" is always the total, the thing you're comparing against. Here it's the 24 marks available. Students mix this up constantly: the part goes on top, the whole goes underneath.

## Type 3: finding the whole from a part

Sometimes you know the part and the percentage, but not the total. For example: "12 students walk to school. That is 30% of the class. How many students are in the class?"

The method: divide the part by the percentage written as a decimal.

### Example: the size of a class

1. Change 30% to a decimal: 0.30
2. Divide the part by it: 12 ÷ 0.30 = 40
3. There are 40 students in the class.

Check it: 30% of 40 is 0.30 × 40 = 12. That matches. When in doubt, run the answer backwards like this. It takes five seconds and catches most errors.

## Quick tricks for mental math

You can do a lot of percentages in your head if you start from 10%.

- **10%**: divide by 10. 10% of 350 is 35.
- **5%**: half of 10%. 5% of 350 is 17.5.
- **20%**: double 10%. 20% of 350 is 70.
- **50%**: divide by 2. 50% of 350 is 175.

Want a 15% tip on a $40 meal? 10% is $4 and 5% is $2, so 15% is $6. No calculator, no awkward silence at the table.

## The mistakes I see every year

- **Forgetting to divide by 100.** 15% of 200 is not 15 × 200 = 3000. Always turn 15% into 0.15 first. This one error probably costs more marks than any other in this topic.
- **Dividing the wrong way round.** For "what percent is 18 of 24", divide 18 ÷ 24, not 24 ÷ 18. The part goes on top.
- **Using the wrong whole.** If a question says "30% of the class", the class is the whole, not the group you already counted.
- **Stopping at the discount.** If a question asks what you *pay*, subtract the discount from the original price. Finding the $20 discount is only half the question.

## Extra worked example: finding the original price

A jacket costs $45 in a sale, after 25% off. What was the original price? This is Type 3 wearing a disguise.

1. If 25% is off, you are paying the other 75%: 100 − 25 = 75.
2. Change 75% to a decimal: 0.75.
3. Divide the part by the decimal: 45 ÷ 0.75 = 60.
4. The original price was $60.

Check: 25% of 60 is 15, and 60 − 15 = 45. It matches.

## Percent vs percentage points

This one confuses even adults, so it's worth learning properly. I once watched a news debate where nobody on the panel got this right.

- **Percentage points** are the simple difference between two percentages.
- **Percent change** is that difference as a share of the starting value.

Say a tax rate goes from 20% to 25%:

1. The rise in percentage points: 25 − 20 = 5 percentage points.
2. The percent change: 5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%.

So the tax went up by 5 percentage points, which is a 25% increase. Both statements are true. They just measure different things. Headlines mix them up all the time, so now you'll spot it.

## Where you'll actually use this

- **Tips.** A $46 meal with a 20% tip: 10% is $4.60, double it to get $9.20. Round to $9 or $10. Nobody minds.
- **Discounts.** A $120 jacket at 30% off: 0.30 × 120 = $36 off, so you pay $84. Or pay 70% directly: 0.70 × 120 = $84.
- **Grades.** A score of 42 out of 50 is 42 ÷ 50 × 100 = 84%.
- **Battery and storage.** "23% battery left" means 23 out of every 100 units of charge. If your phone lasted 8 hours on a full charge, 25% left is roughly 2 more hours.

## Mini FAQs

**Can a percentage be more than 100%?**
Yes. Percentages over 100% just mean "more than the whole". 150% of 40 is 1.5 × 40 = 60. A plant that grows from 20 cm to 50 cm has grown by 150%: (50 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = 150.

**What is the difference between 0.5 and 0.5%?**
A lot! 0.5 is the same as 50%, while 0.5% is the same as 0.005. The percent sign means "divide by 100", so never drop it casually.

**How do I find 1% of something quickly?**
Divide by 100. Just move the decimal point two places left. 1% of 250 is 2.5. This is handy because once you know 1%, you can build any percentage: 7% of 250 is 7 × 2.5 = 17.5.
