The mean is what most people mean when they say "the average". One number that sums up a whole list.

Here's the picture I always start with. Five friends pool their money and share it out equally. The amount each person ends up with, that's the mean. Simple as that.

## The method

**mean = sum of all the values ÷ number of values**

Two steps, that's all:

1. Add up all the numbers.
2. Divide by how many numbers there are.

## Example: test scores

Maya's last five math test scores were 72, 85, 90, 68 and 80. What's her mean score?

1. Add them up: 72 + 85 + 90 + 68 + 80 = 395
2. Count them: there are 5 scores.
3. Divide: 395 ÷ 5 = 79
4. Her mean score is 79.

Notice something? 79 isn't one of her actual scores. That's completely normal. The mean is a balance point, not a value from the list. Students find this weird the first time, then they get used to it.

## Example: temperatures over a week

The highest temperature each day for a week, in °C, was 18, 21, 19, 23, 20, 22 and 17.

1. Add them up: 18 + 21 + 19 + 23 + 20 + 22 + 17 = 140
2. Count them: 7 days.
3. Divide: 140 ÷ 7 = 20
4. The mean high temperature was 20 °C.

## Yes, the mean can be a decimal

Sam read 3, 5, 2 and 4 books over four months.

1. Sum: 3 + 5 + 2 + 4 = 14
2. Count: 4
3. Mean: 14 ÷ 4 = 3.5

Sam read a mean of 3.5 books a month. No, there was no month with exactly 3.5 books. Nobody reads half a book and stops (well, almost nobody). The mean is still a useful summary even when it's not a "real" value.

## Negative numbers work too

Winter temperatures over five mornings were −3, 2, 5, −1 and 4 °C.

1. Sum: −3 + 2 + 5 + (−1) + 4 = 7
2. Count: 5
3. Mean: 7 ÷ 5 = 1.4 °C

The only tricky bit is the adding. Take your time with the signs. −3 + 2 is −1, not 5. I watch students rush this step more than any other.

## Working backwards: finding a missing value

Sometimes you know the mean you want and need to figure out one missing number. This is where the mean gets fun, because you run the whole process in reverse.

Leo wants a mean of 85 across four tests. His first three scores are 80, 88 and 82. What does he need on the fourth test?

1. Find the total he needs: 85 × 4 = 340
2. Add the scores he already has: 80 + 88 + 82 = 250
3. Subtract: 340 − 250 = 90
4. He needs 90 on the last test.

Check: (80 + 88 + 82 + 90) ÷ 4 = 340 ÷ 4 = 85. Correct.

The trick is remembering that mean × count = total. Once you have the total, everything else is just subtraction.

## The mean from a frequency table

A soccer team kept track of how many goals it scored in each of its 14 matches.

| Goals in a match | Number of matches |
|---|---|
| 0 | 3 |
| 1 | 5 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 2 |

You could write out all 14 values. Or you could be smart about it.

1. Multiply each goal value by how many times it happened: 0 × 3 = 0, 1 × 5 = 5, 2 × 4 = 8, 3 × 2 = 6
2. Add these to get the total goals: 0 + 5 + 8 + 6 = 19
3. Add the matches: 3 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 14
4. Divide: 19 ÷ 14 ≈ 1.36
5. The team scored a mean of about 1.36 goals per match.

## When the mean lies to you

The mean uses every single number, so one extreme value can drag it a long way off. Watch this.

Five friends get this much pocket money a week: $5, $6, $5, $7 and $42.

The mean is (5 + 6 + 5 + 7 + 42) ÷ 5 = 65 ÷ 5 = $13. But four of the five friends get $7 or less! That $42 is doing all the damage.

This is exactly why you need to know the median too. In lopsided data like this, the median tells a fairer story. I go into the whole comparison in [mean vs median](/articles/mean-vs-median), and it's worth the read.

## Mistakes I see every year

- **Dividing by the wrong count.** A zero still counts as a value. The list 4, 0, 5 has three values, so the mean is 9 ÷ 3 = 3, not 9 ÷ 2.
- **Dividing by the number of rows in a frequency table.** In the soccer example, divide by 14 matches, not by the 4 rows. This one gets almost everyone the first time.
- **Rounding too early.** Add everything up, divide, and only round at the very end. Early rounding is how small errors sneak in.
- **Adding negatives as positives.** −3 + 2 is −1, not 5. Slow down on the signs.

## Example: mean monthly spending

Priya tracked what she spent on snacks each month: $45, $60, $52, $48 and $55. What was her mean monthly spending?

1. Add them up: 45 + 60 + 52 + 48 + 55 = 260
2. Count them: 5 months.
3. Divide: 260 ÷ 5 = 52
4. Her mean spending was $52 a month.

Now she can actually use that number. Setting aside $52 a month should cover her snack habit on average. That's the whole point of a mean: one number you can plan around.

## Mean, median and mode at a glance

| | Mean | Median | Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| How to find it | Add all values, divide by the count | Order the values, take the middle one | Take the value that appears most often |
| Uses every value | Yes | No | No |
| Can be a decimal | Yes | Yes, for an even count | No, always one of the values |
| Best when | No extreme values | There are outliers | You want the most popular choice |

Take Priya's snack spending (45, 60, 52, 48, 55). The mean is 52. The median is also 52 (the middle of 45, 48, 52, 55, 60). And there's no mode, because every value appears exactly once. When all three agree like this, you can trust the number.

## Where you'll actually use the mean

- **Grades.** A final grade is often the mean of your test scores. That's why one bad test can pull the whole thing down, and why teachers drop the lowest score sometimes.
- **Sports.** A batter's average, a runner's mean time per race. Fans compare players across whole seasons with a single number.
- **Weather.** The "average July temperature" in a forecast is the mean of many years of daily highs.
- **Money.** A mean monthly bill helps you budget, even though no single month matches it exactly.

Here's a fun fact to drop in conversation: whenever you see the word "average" in the news, it's almost always the mean.

## Quick questions

**Can the mean be negative?**

Sure. If most of the values are negative, the sum is negative and so is the mean. For −8, −4 and −3: the sum is −15, and −15 ÷ 3 = −5.

**What if the list has only one number?**

Then the mean is that number. For the list [9]: the sum is 9, there's 1 value, and 9 ÷ 1 = 9. Trivial, but good to know the method doesn't break.

**My mean came out as a long decimal. Did I mess up?**

Probably not. Means are often not whole numbers, like 3.5 books a month. Just don't round until the final step, and only round if the question asks for it.

## Try it yourself

1. Find the mean of 12, 15, 9, 14 and 10.
2. The mean of three numbers is 20. Two of them are 18 and 25. What is the third?

**Answers:** 1) The sum is 60, and 60 ÷ 5 = 12. 2) The total must be 20 × 3 = 60, and 60 − 18 − 25 = 17.
