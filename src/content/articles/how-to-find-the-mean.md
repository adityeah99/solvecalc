The mean is what most people mean when they say "the average". It gives you one number that sums up a whole list of numbers.

Here is the idea. If five friends pooled their money and shared it out equally, the amount each person would get is the mean.

## The method

**mean = sum of all the values ÷ number of values**

In two steps:

1. Add up all the numbers.
2. Divide by how many numbers there are.

## Example: test scores

Maya's last five math test scores were 72, 85, 90, 68 and 80. What is her mean score?

1. Add them up: 72 + 85 + 90 + 68 + 80 = 395
2. Count them: there are 5 scores.
3. Divide: 395 ÷ 5 = 79
4. Her mean score is 79.

Notice that 79 is not one of her actual scores. That is normal. The mean is a balance point, not necessarily a value in the list.

## Example: temperatures over a week

The highest temperature each day for a week, in °C, was 18, 21, 19, 23, 20, 22 and 17.

1. Add them up: 18 + 21 + 19 + 23 + 20 + 22 + 17 = 140
2. Count them: 7 days.
3. Divide: 140 ÷ 7 = 20
4. The mean high temperature was 20 °C.

## The mean can be a decimal

Sam read 3, 5, 2 and 4 books over four months.

1. Sum: 3 + 5 + 2 + 4 = 14
2. Count: 4
3. Mean: 14 ÷ 4 = 3.5

Sam read a mean of 3.5 books a month. There was no month where Sam read exactly 3.5 books, but the mean is still a useful summary.

## Negative numbers work too

Winter temperatures over five mornings were −3, 2, 5, −1 and 4 °C.

1. Sum: −3 + 2 + 5 + (−1) + 4 = 7
2. Count: 5
3. Mean: 7 ÷ 5 = 1.4 °C

Be careful to subtract the negative values when you add them up.

## Finding a missing value

Sometimes you know the mean you want and need to work out one missing number.

Leo wants a mean of 85 across four tests. His first three scores are 80, 88 and 82. What does he need on the fourth test?

1. Find the total he needs: 85 × 4 = 340
2. Add the scores he already has: 80 + 88 + 82 = 250
3. Subtract: 340 − 250 = 90
4. He needs 90 on the last test.

Check: (80 + 88 + 82 + 90) ÷ 4 = 340 ÷ 4 = 85. Correct.

## The mean from a frequency table

A soccer team kept track of how many goals it scored in each of its 14 matches.

| Goals in a match | Number of matches |
|---|---|
| 0 | 3 |
| 1 | 5 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 2 |

You could write out all 14 values, but there is a faster way.

1. Multiply each goal value by how many times it happened: 0 × 3 = 0, 1 × 5 = 5, 2 × 4 = 8, 3 × 2 = 6
2. Add these to get the total goals: 0 + 5 + 8 + 6 = 19
3. Add the matches: 3 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 14
4. Divide: 19 ÷ 14 ≈ 1.36
5. The team scored a mean of about 1.36 goals per match.

## When the mean can mislead

The mean uses every number, so one very large or very small value can pull it a long way. Suppose five friends get this much pocket money a week: $5, $6, $5, $7 and $42.

The mean is (5 + 6 + 5 + 7 + 42) ÷ 5 = 65 ÷ 5 = $13. But four of the five friends get $7 or less. The $42 drags the mean up.

In cases like this, the median can be a better summary. You can read more in [mean vs median](/articles/mean-vs-median).

## Mistakes to watch for

- **Dividing by the wrong count.** If a value is 0, it still counts. The list 4, 0, 5 has three values, so the mean is 9 ÷ 3 = 3.
- **Dividing by the number of rows in a frequency table.** In the soccer example, divide by 14 matches, not by the 4 rows.
- **Rounding too early.** Add everything up first, divide, and only round at the very end.
- **Adding negatives as positives.** −3 + 2 is −1, not 5.

## Try it yourself

1. Find the mean of 12, 15, 9, 14 and 10.
2. The mean of three numbers is 20. Two of them are 18 and 25. What is the third?

**Answers:** 1) The sum is 60, and 60 ÷ 5 = 12. 2) The total must be 20 × 3 = 60, and 60 − 18 − 25 = 17.
