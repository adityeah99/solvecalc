I once watched a student answer a perimeter question perfectly. Correct numbers, correct steps. Then he wrote "m²" at the end. One tiny unit, whole mark gone. Perimeter trips people up in silly ways, so let us nail it properly.

The perimeter of a shape is the total distance around its edge. Walk all the way around a field and the distance you covered is its perimeter.

You need it whenever you measure something that goes around the outside: a fence for a garden, a frame for a picture, trim around a room.

## The formula

A rectangle has four sides. The two long sides match, and the two short sides match. We call them the **length** (l) and the **width** (w).

Add all four sides:

**P = l + w + l + w**

Two of each, so this shortens to:

**P = 2 × (l + w)**

Same answer, less writing. Use the short one.

## Example: fencing a garden

A vegetable garden is 8 metres long and 5 metres wide. How much fencing goes all the way around?

1. Add length and width: 8 + 5 = 13
2. Double it: 2 × 13 = 26
3. You need 26 metres of fencing.

Check by adding all four sides: 8 + 5 + 8 + 5 = 26. Same answer, as it should be.

## Example: trim around a bedroom

A bedroom floor is 4.5 m long and 3.2 m wide. How much skirting board runs around the edge, ignoring the door?

1. Add length and width: 4.5 + 3.2 = 7.7
2. Double it: 2 × 7.7 = 15.4
3. You need 15.4 m of skirting board.

Decimals behave exactly like whole numbers here. Nothing special happens.

## Example: sides in different units

A poster is 2 m tall and 75 cm wide. What is its perimeter?

Stop. You cannot add metres and centimetres directly. Convert first, then add.

1. Change 2 m to centimetres: 2 × 100 = 200 cm
2. Add length and width: 200 + 75 = 275
3. Double it: 2 × 275 = 550
4. The perimeter is 550 cm, which is 5.5 m.

Mixed units are a trap examiners love. Convert first, always.

## Squares are a special case

A square is a rectangle with all four sides equal. If each side is s:

**P = 4 × s**

A square tile with 30 cm sides has a perimeter of 4 × 30 = 120 cm.

## Working backwards: finding a missing side

Sometimes you know the perimeter and one side, and the other side is hiding.

A dog pen has a perimeter of 36 m and is 11 m long. How wide is it?

1. Halve the perimeter. That gives one length plus one width: 36 ÷ 2 = 18
2. Take away the length: 18 − 11 = 7
3. The pen is 7 m wide.

Check: 2 × (11 + 7) = 2 × 18 = 36. Correct.

## Perimeter is not area

This is the big one, and the mistake from my intro. Perimeter measures the edge. Area measures the space inside. Two rectangles can share a perimeter and hold wildly different areas:

| Rectangle | Perimeter | Area |
|---|---|---|
| 6 m by 4 m | 2 × (6 + 4) = 20 m | 6 × 4 = 24 m² |
| 9 m by 1 m | 2 × (9 + 1) = 20 m | 9 × 1 = 9 m² |

Both need 20 m of fence, but the first holds far more space. Watch the units too. Perimeter is a length: m. Area is square units: m². That little ² is worth a mark.

Curious about area for a curved shape? Read [how to find the area of a circle](/articles/area-of-a-circle).

## Mistakes to watch for

- **Adding only two sides.** 8 + 5 = 13 is only half the journey. Double it.
- **Mixing units.** Convert everything to one unit before adding. No exceptions.
- **Multiplying instead of adding.** 8 × 5 gives the area, not the perimeter. Different question, different operation.
- **Writing square units.** Perimeter is a distance: m or cm, never m² or cm². Remember my student.

## Quick reference: perimeters of common shapes

Rectangles are not the only shape with a perimeter. The ones you will meet most:

| Shape | Perimeter formula | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Square | P = 4 × s | four equal sides |
| Rectangle | P = 2 × (l + w) | twice length plus width |
| Triangle | P = a + b + c | add all three sides |
| Circle | P = 2 × π × r | called the **circumference** |

The idea never changes: add up every side. The circle is the odd one out, no straight sides, so we use π (about 3.14) instead.

## Example: fencing with a gate

A garden is 12 m long and 9 m wide. You want to fence it fully but leave a 1.5 m gap for the gate. How much fencing do you need?

1. Full perimeter first: 2 × (12 + 9) = 2 × 21 = 42 m.
2. Subtract the gate gap: 42 − 1.5 = 40.5 m.
3. You need 40.5 m of fencing.

Real projects nearly always have a gap: a doorway in the skirting, a driveway opening in a fence. Full perimeter first, then subtract the gaps.

## When you double the sides

Double the sides of a rectangle and the perimeter doubles. But look at the area:

| Rectangle | Perimeter | Area |
|---|---|---|
| 4 m by 6 m | 2 × (4 + 6) = 20 m | 4 × 6 = 24 m² |
| 8 m by 12 m | 2 × (8 + 12) = 40 m | 8 × 12 = 96 m² |

Perimeter went 20 to 40, doubled. Area went 24 to 96, quadrupled. Perimeter grows with the sides. Area grows with the square of the sides. This is why a slightly bigger pizza gives you much more food for only a little extra crust. Worth knowing next time you order.

## Quick questions

**Must I use the same units everywhere?**

Yes. You cannot add 2 m and 75 cm directly. Convert to one unit first, then add, exactly like the poster example above.

**Can two shapes share a perimeter but not an area?**

Yes. The 6-by-4 and 9-by-1 rectangles above both have a 20 m perimeter, with areas of 24 m² and 9 m². Fun fact: of all rectangles with a fixed perimeter, the square holds the most area.

**Does a circle's perimeter have a special name?**

It is called the circumference. The formula is C = 2 × π × r, where r is the radius. A circle with radius 5 cm has a circumference of about 2 × 3.14 × 5 = 31.4 cm.

## Try it yourself

1. Find the perimeter of a rectangle 12 cm long and 7 cm wide.
2. A rectangle has a perimeter of 50 m and a width of 10 m. How long is it?

**Answers:** 1) 2 × (12 + 7) = 2 × 19 = 38 cm. 2) 50 ÷ 2 = 25, then 25 − 10 = 15 m.
