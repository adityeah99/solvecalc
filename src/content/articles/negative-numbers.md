A negative number is any number less than zero, written with a minus sign in front, like −3 or −12. They show up in temperatures, bank balances, and heights below sea level.

Thinking of a number line helps a lot. Positive numbers sit to the right of zero and negative numbers to the left. Moving right makes a number bigger; moving left makes it smaller.

## Adding and subtracting

Picture the number line. Adding moves you right, and subtracting moves you left. This works even when you start on a negative number.

### Worked example: −5 + 8

Start at −5. Adding 8 means moving 8 steps to the right.

−5 → −4 → −3 → −2 → −1 → 0 → 1 → 2 → 3

You land on 3, so −5 + 8 = 3.

### Worked example: −3 − 4

Start at −3. Subtracting 4 means moving 4 steps to the left, further into the negatives.

−3 → −4 → −5 → −6 → −7

You land on −7, so −3 − 4 = −7.

### Subtracting a negative

Subtracting a negative is the same as adding. Two minus signs next to each other turn into a plus. So 6 − (−2) becomes 6 + 2 = 8. Think of it as removing a debt, which leaves you better off.

## Multiplying and dividing

For multiplication and division, look only at the signs. The size of the answer comes from the normal times table; the sign follows two simple rules:

- Same signs give a **positive** answer.
- Different signs give a **negative** answer.

### Worked example: −6 × −4

The numbers give 6 × 4 = 24. Both signs are negative, and same signs give a positive. So −6 × −4 = 24.

### Worked example: −20 ÷ 5

The numbers give 20 ÷ 5 = 4. The signs are different (one negative, one positive), so the answer is negative: −20 ÷ 5 = −4.

## A quick sign summary

| Operation | Signs | Result |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| −4 + 6    | —     | 2      |
| −4 − 6    | —     | −10    |
| −3 × 5    | different | −15 |
| −3 × −5   | same  | 15     |
| 12 ÷ −4   | different | −3  |
| −12 ÷ −4  | same  | 3      |

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the sign rules for adding with those for multiplying.** Two negatives multiplied give a positive, but two negatives added stay negative: −4 + −4 = −8.
- **Losing a minus sign.** Write signs clearly. −5 − 3 is −8, not 8.
- **Misreading a double negative.** 7 − (−2) is 7 + 2 = 9, not 5.
- **Rushing the size.** Sort out the sign first, then do the ordinary calculation with the digits.

## Negative numbers in real life

Negative numbers are not just classroom maths. You meet them all the time:

- **Temperature.** If it is −4°C at night and warms up by 9 degrees by the afternoon, the new temperature is −4 + 9 = 5°C.
- **Money.** Imagine your account shows −20, meaning you owe 20. If you pay back 8, your balance becomes −20 + 8 = −12. You still owe 12, but less than before.
- **Height.** A submarine at −120 m (120 metres below sea level) rises 45 m. Its new depth is −120 + 45 = −75 m.

In each case the maths is the same as on the number line. Only the story changes.

## Comparing negative numbers

With negatives, the number closer to zero is always bigger. So −3 is bigger than −8, even though 8 looks like the bigger digit. A quick way to check: on the number line, the number further right wins.

Put these in order from smallest to biggest: 3, −10, −4, 0.

- −10 is furthest left, so it is smallest.
- Then −4, then 0, then 3.

The order is −10, −4, 0, 3. The most common slip is thinking −10 is bigger than −4 because 10 > 4. With negatives, flip that instinct.

## Worked example: a two-step sum

Solve −12 + 5 × −2.

Multiplication comes before addition (see the [order of operations](/articles/order-of-operations)).

1. Do the multiplication first: 5 × −2 = −10. The signs are different, so the answer is negative.
2. Now the sum is −12 + −10.
3. Adding two negatives stays negative: −12 + −10 = −22.

The answer is −22.

## Quick questions

**Is zero positive or negative?**

Neither. Zero sits exactly between the positives and the negatives, so it has no sign.

**Can an answer be negative in real life?**

Yes, whenever the question allows owing or going below a starting point. A bank balance of −30 means you owe 30. A temperature of −5°C is perfectly real.

**Why is a negative times a negative positive?**

Watch the pattern: 3 × −4 = −12, 2 × −4 = −8, 1 × −4 = −4, 0 × −4 = 0. Each step the answer goes up by 4. Keep going: −1 × −4 = 4. The pattern only works if the answer turns positive.

## Putting it all together

Real sums mix everything: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division in one line. Work left to right within each rank, and handle the signs as you go.

### Worked example: −3 − (−7) + (−2)

1. Turn the double negative into a plus: −3 − (−7) becomes −3 + 7.
2. Add: −3 + 7 = 4.
3. Now add the last part: 4 + (−2) = 2.

The answer is 2.

### Worked example: 15 ÷ −3 × −2

Division and multiplication are equal in rank, so go left to right.

1. 15 ÷ −3 = −5. Different signs give a negative.
2. −5 × −2 = 10. Same signs give a positive.

The answer is 10. Notice how the sign flips at each step. If you lose track, slow down and do one operation at a time.

## Try it yourself

1. Work out −7 + 12.
2. Work out (−8) × (−3).

**Answers:** 1) Start at −7 and move 12 right, landing on 5. 2) Same signs give positive, and 8 × 3 = 24, so the answer is 24.
