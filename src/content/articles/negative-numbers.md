First thing I tell my students about negative numbers: stop being scared of the minus sign. It is just direction. A negative number is any number less than zero, written with a minus in front, like −3 or −12. You already meet them daily: temperatures below freezing, a bank balance in the red, heights below sea level.

Draw a number line in your head. Positives live to the right of zero, negatives to the left. Walk right and the number gets bigger. Walk left and it gets smaller. That one picture handles nearly everything in this article.

## Adding and subtracting: just walk the line

Here is the trick I teach on day one. Adding means walk right. Subtracting means walk left. Works even when you start in negative territory.

### Worked example: −5 + 8

Start at −5. Adding 8 means 8 steps right.

−5 → −4 → −3 → −2 → −1 → 0 → 1 → 2 → 3

You land on 3, so −5 + 8 = 3.

### Worked example: −3 − 4

Start at −3. Subtracting 4 means 4 steps left, deeper into the negatives.

−3 → −4 → −5 → −6 → −7

You land on −7, so −3 − 4 = −7.

### Subtracting a negative

This trips up almost everyone the first time. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding. Two minus signs side by side turn into a plus: 6 − (−2) becomes 6 + 2 = 8.

Think of it as wiping out a debt. If someone removes a debt of 2 from your account, you are 2 better off. Same maths, friendlier story.

## Multiplying and dividing: check the signs first

For multiplication and division, ignore the minus signs for a moment. Do the ordinary times-table part, then fix the sign with two rules:

- Same signs give a **positive** answer.
- Different signs give a **negative** answer.

That is the whole rule. Now let me be blunt, because I watch students mix this up every single year: adding and multiplying play by different rules. Two negatives added stay negative. Two negatives multiplied turn positive. Say that sentence to yourself until it sticks.

### Worked example: −6 × −4

The digits give 6 × 4 = 24. Both signs are negative. Same signs, so the answer is positive: −6 × −4 = 24.

### Worked example: −20 ÷ 5

The digits give 20 ÷ 5 = 4. One negative, one positive. Different signs, so the answer is negative: −20 ÷ 5 = −4.

## A quick sign summary

| Operation | Signs | Result |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| −4 + 6    | n/a   | 2      |
| −4 − 6    | n/a   | −10    |
| −3 × 5    | different | −15 |
| −3 × −5   | same  | 15     |
| 12 ÷ −4   | different | −3  |
| −12 ÷ −4  | same  | 3      |

## Mistakes I see every year

- **Using the adding rules for multiplying.** Two negatives multiplied give a positive, but two negatives added stay negative: −4 + −4 = −8. Different operations, different rules.
- **Dropping a minus sign halfway through.** Write your signs clearly and check them at the end. −5 − 3 is −8, not 8. Most "I got it wrong" moments are really "I lost a sign" moments.
- **Misreading a double negative.** 7 − (−2) is 7 + 2 = 9, not 5. When two minuses meet, they become a plus.
- **Rushing the digits.** Sort out the sign first, then do the ordinary calculation with the digits. One job at a time.

## Where negatives actually show up

Negative numbers are not just classroom maths. They are hiding in your normal day:

- **Temperature.** It is −4°C at night and warms up by 9 degrees by afternoon. The new temperature is −4 + 9 = 5°C.
- **Money.** Your account shows −20, meaning you owe 20. You pay back 8, so the balance becomes −20 + 8 = −12. You still owe 12, but less than before.
- **Height.** A submarine sits at −120 m, that is 120 metres below sea level, then rises 45 m. Its new depth is −120 + 45 = −75 m.

Every one of these is the same number-line walk. Only the story changes.

## Comparing negatives: flip your instinct

With negatives, the number closer to zero is always bigger. So −3 is bigger than −8, even though 8 looks like the bigger digit. On the number line, the number further right wins. Every time.

Try ordering these from smallest to biggest: 3, −10, −4, 0.

- −10 is furthest left, so it is smallest.
- Then −4, then 0, then 3.

The order is −10, −4, 0, 3. The classic slip is thinking −10 beats −4 because 10 > 4. With negatives, flip that instinct around.

## Worked example: a two-step sum

Solve −12 + 5 × −2.

Multiplication comes before addition (see the [order of operations](/articles/order-of-operations)).

1. Do the multiplication first: 5 × −2 = −10. Different signs, so negative.
2. Now the sum is −12 + −10.
3. Adding two negatives stays negative: −12 + −10 = −22.

The answer is −22.

## Quick questions

**Is zero positive or negative?**

Neither. Zero sits exactly between the positives and the negatives, so it has no sign at all.

**Can an answer be negative in real life?**

Yes, whenever the question allows owing or going below a starting point. A bank balance of −30 means you owe 30. A temperature of −5°C is perfectly real. Negatives describe real situations, not broken maths.

**Why is a negative times a negative positive?**

Watch the pattern and trust it: 3 × −4 = −12, 2 × −4 = −8, 1 × −4 = −4, 0 × −4 = 0. Each step, the answer climbs by 4. Keep going one more step: −1 × −4 = 4. The pattern only holds together if the answer turns positive. Maths is consistent like that.

## Putting it all together

Real sums mix everything: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division in one line. Work left to right within each rank, and handle the signs as you go.

### Worked example: −3 − (−7) + (−2)

1. Turn the double negative into a plus: −3 − (−7) becomes −3 + 7.
2. Add: −3 + 7 = 4.
3. Now add the last part: 4 + (−2) = 2.

The answer is 2.

### Worked example: 15 ÷ −3 × −2

Division and multiplication are equal in rank, so go left to right.

1. 15 ÷ −3 = −5. Different signs give a negative.
2. −5 × −2 = 10. Same signs give a positive.

The answer is 10. Notice how the sign flips at each step. If you lose track, slow down and do one operation at a time.

## Try it yourself

1. Work out −7 + 12.
2. Work out (−8) × (−3).

**Answers:** 1) Start at −7 and walk 12 right, landing on 5. 2) Same signs give positive, and 8 × 3 = 24, so the answer is 24.
