An equation is a statement that two things are equal. 2x + 3 = 11 says "two lots of some number, plus 3" is the same as 11. Simple enough.

A **linear equation** is one of the simplest kinds you'll meet. The letter (the variable) appears on its own. Never squared. Never multiplied by itself. Never hiding on the bottom of a fraction.

## What makes an equation linear

Every variable has a power of 1. You see x, but not x², x³ or √x. That's the whole test.

Some linear equations:

- 2x + 3 = 11
- y = 3x − 2
- x/4 = 5
- 7 − a = 2a + 1

Some that are **not** linear:

- x² + 1 = 10 (x is squared)
- 1/x = 2 (x is on the bottom of a fraction)
- √x = 3 (x is under a square root)
- xy = 12 (two variables multiplied together)

The letter doesn't have to be x. It can be a, t, n, whatever. What matters is how it shows up, not what it's called.

## Why "linear"?

Take a linear equation with two variables, like y = 2x + 1, and plot it. Every point lands on a straight line. That's where the name comes from. Line. Linear.

Try some x values and watch y:

| x | y = 2x + 1 |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 7 |

Each time x goes up by 1, y goes up by 2. Steady change, straight line. The 2 in front of x is the **slope** (how steep the line is), and the + 1 is where the line crosses the y-axis. My students remember it as: slope is the tilt, the constant is the starting point.

## A real one: the taxi ride

A taxi charges $3 to start the ride plus $2 for every kilometre. If k is the kilometres, the cost C is:

C = 2k + 3

Linear. Every extra kilometre adds the same $2. No surprises.

### Example: how much does a 5 km ride cost?

1. Put k = 5 in: C = 2 × 5 + 3
2. Multiply first: 2 × 5 = 10
3. Then add: 10 + 3 = 13
4. The ride costs $13.

### Example: how far can you go for $19?

Now you know the cost and want the distance. Work backwards.

1. Write the equation: 2k + 3 = 19
2. Take 3 from both sides: 2k = 16
3. Divide both sides by 2: k = 8
4. You can travel 8 km.

Finding the letter's value like this is called **solving** the equation. There's a full guide in [how to solve a linear equation](/articles/how-to-solve-a-linear-equation).

## Checking a solution

A solution is a value that makes both sides equal. Don't trust it until you check. Put it back in.

Is x = 4 a solution of 2x + 3 = 11?

1. Left side: 2 × 4 + 3 = 8 + 3 = 11
2. Right side: 11
3. Both sides are 11. Yes, x = 4 works.

Is x = 5 a solution? Left side: 2 × 5 + 3 = 13. That's not 11, so no. I make my students check every answer this way. It takes ten seconds and catches half of all mistakes.

## How many solutions can there be?

Usually exactly one. But there are two weird cases, and exams love them:

- **No solution.** 2x + 1 = 2x + 5 can never be true. Adding 1 to a number can never give the same result as adding 5. The x's cancel and you're left with nonsense.
- **Every number works.** 2(x + 1) = 2x + 2 is true for any x, because both sides are the same thing in different clothes.

## The mistakes I see every year

- **Assuming any equation with x is linear.** Look at the power, not the letter. x² = 16 has an x, but it's a [quadratic equation](/articles/what-is-a-quadratic-equation), not a linear one.
- **Reading 3x as "thirty-something".** 3x means 3 × x. When x = 4, 3x is 12, not 34. This one is more common than you'd think.
- **Adding before multiplying.** In 2 × 5 + 3, multiply first. The answer is 13, not 16.

## Linear vs quadratic: spot the difference

These two get mixed up constantly. Here's the two-second test:

| | Linear | Quadratic |
|---|---|---|
| Highest power of x | 1 | 2 |
| Example | 2x + 3 = 11 | x² − 5x + 6 = 0 |
| Graph shape | Straight line | U-shaped curve (parabola) |
| Number of solutions | Usually one | Up to two |

Look for x². If you see a squared variable, it's quadratic, no matter how simple the rest looks. x² = 16 looks harmless, but it has two answers (4 and −4). That's the giveaway.

Here's a neat way to think about it: every quadratic is "almost linear". Cover up the x² term in x² + 2x = 8 and what's left (2x = 8) is linear. The squared term is the entire story. It bends the line into a curve and doubles the answers.

## The three forms of a linear equation

You'll meet the same line dressed three ways. All linear:

| Form | Looks like | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Slope-intercept | y = mx + b | Reading the slope and y-intercept straight off |
| Standard | ax + by = c | Tidying up and solving systems of equations |
| Point-slope | y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) | Writing the equation when you know a point and the slope |

Example: y = 2x + 1 is slope-intercept form (m = 2, b = 1). The same line in standard form is 2x − y = −1. Different clothes, same line. Students panic when the form changes, but it's just the same equation wearing a different outfit.

## Worked example: is y = x² + 2x + 1 linear?

1. Scan the powers: the highest power of x is 2 (in x²).
2. Linear allows only power 1.
3. So no. This is **quadratic**. Parabola, not a line.

## Two quick questions

**Is x = 5 a linear equation?**
Yes. No x² anywhere, and its graph is a vertical straight line through x = 5. A boring-looking equation can still be linear. Simplicity doesn't disqualify it.

**Can a linear equation multiply two variables, like xy = 4?**
No. The moment two variables multiply each other, it's not linear anymore. The graph becomes a curve.

## Try it yourself

1. Is 5x − 7 = 3 linear? If so, solve it.
2. A gym charges a $20 sign-up fee plus $15 a month. Write the cost after m months, then find the cost for 6 months.

**Answers:** 1) Yes, it's linear. 5x = 10, so x = 2. 2) C = 15m + 20. For 6 months, 15 × 6 + 20 = 110, so $110.
