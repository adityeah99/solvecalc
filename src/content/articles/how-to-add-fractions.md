A fraction tells you how many equal pieces you have out of a whole. In 3/8, the bottom number (the denominator) says the whole is cut into 8 pieces, and the top number (the numerator) says you have 3 of them.

Adding fractions is easy when the pieces are the same size. When they're not, you first cut them into pieces that match. That single idea is the entire article. Everything below is just details.

## Adding fractions with the same denominator

Imagine a pizza cut into 8 equal slices. You eat 2 slices and your friend eats 3. Together you ate 2/8 + 3/8 of the pizza.

The slices are all the same size, so you just count them:

1. Check the denominators. Both are 8, so they match.
2. Add the numerators: 2 + 3 = 5
3. Keep the denominator the same: 5/8

Together you ate 5/8 of the pizza. The denominator stays at 8 because the slices are still eighths. You never add the bottom numbers. Never.

## Adding fractions with different denominators

Now try 1/3 + 1/4. A third and a quarter are different sized pieces, so you can't just count them. You need a common denominator: a number that both 3 and 4 divide into.

The best choice is the **least common multiple** (LCM) of the denominators. The multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, 15… and the multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16… The first number on both lists is 12.

### Example: 1/3 + 1/4

1. Find the LCM of 3 and 4: it is 12.
2. Rewrite 1/3 with denominator 12. Since 3 × 4 = 12, multiply top and bottom by 4: 1/3 = 4/12
3. Rewrite 1/4 with denominator 12. Since 4 × 3 = 12, multiply top and bottom by 3: 1/4 = 3/12
4. Add the numerators: 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12

So 1/3 + 1/4 = 7/12.

Multiplying the top and bottom by the same number doesn't change the value of a fraction. 4/12 is exactly the same amount as 1/3, just cut into smaller pieces. This is the step students rush. Don't rush it.

### Example: 5/6 + 3/4

1. Find the LCM of 6 and 4. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18… Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12… The LCM is 12.
2. Rewrite 5/6: multiply top and bottom by 2 to get 10/12
3. Rewrite 3/4: multiply top and bottom by 3 to get 9/12
4. Add: 10/12 + 9/12 = 19/12
5. The top is bigger than the bottom, so you can write it as a mixed number. 12 goes into 19 once with 7 left over, so 19/12 = 1 7/12.

## Adding mixed numbers

A mixed number, like 2 1/2, has a whole-number part and a fraction part. One easy way to add them is to add the wholes and the fractions separately.

### Example: 2 1/2 + 1 2/3

1. Add the whole numbers: 2 + 1 = 3
2. Add the fractions: 1/2 + 2/3. The LCM of 2 and 3 is 6, so this is 3/6 + 4/6 = 7/6
3. 7/6 is more than one whole. It equals 1 1/6.
4. Add everything: 3 + 1 1/6 = 4 1/6

So 2 1/2 + 1 2/3 = 4 1/6.

## Simplify at the end

After adding, check if your answer can be simplified. For example:

- 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6
- 3 and 6 can both be divided by 3, so 3/6 = 1/2

Teachers notice unsimplified answers. 3/6 is correct, but 1/2 is the finished answer. If you want more practice with this step, read [how to simplify fractions](/articles/how-to-simplify-fractions).

## The mistakes I see on every test

I mark a lot of fraction papers, and the same errors show up again and again:

- **Adding the denominators.** 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. Think about it: half a pizza plus a third of a pizza is clearly more than half, but 2/5 is *less* than half. Your common sense should scream here. The right answer is 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.
- **Changing only the bottom number.** When you turn 1/3 into twelfths, the top must change too. 1/3 = 4/12, not 1/12. Whatever you do to the bottom, do to the top.
- **Using a common denominator that isn't common.** Make sure every denominator divides into the one you choose. Test it before you start rewriting.
- **Forgetting to simplify.** 3/6 is correct, but 1/2 is the neater final answer. Finish the job.

### Example: adding three fractions

The same method works for three or more fractions. Try 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6.

1. Find a number all three denominators divide into. The LCM of 2, 3 and 6 is 6.
2. Rewrite each one: 1/2 = 3/6, 1/3 = 2/6, 1/6 stays as 1/6.
3. Add the numerators: 3/6 + 2/6 + 1/6 = 6/6.
4. 6/6 = 1.

So 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1. When the pieces fit together into a whole, that's a good sign your arithmetic is right.

## Subtracting works the same way

Subtraction is the mirror image of addition: same denominators, same common-denominator method, but you subtract the top numbers instead of adding them.

### Example: 3/4 − 1/3

1. Find the LCM of 4 and 3: it is 12.
2. Rewrite 3/4: multiply top and bottom by 3 to get 9/12.
3. Rewrite 1/3: multiply top and bottom by 4 to get 4/12.
4. Subtract the numerators: 9/12 − 4/12 = 5/12.

So 3/4 − 1/3 = 5/12. Check with decimals: 0.75 − 0.333… = 0.4166…, and 5/12 ≈ 0.4167. It matches.

## Where you'll actually use this

- **Cooking.** A recipe needs 3/4 cup of sugar and 1/3 cup of butter. Together that is 9/12 + 4/12 = 13/12 = 1 1/12 cups.
- **Carpentry.** A board is 2 1/2 feet long and you cut off 3/4 foot. Left over: 5/2 − 3/4 = 10/4 − 3/4 = 7/4 = 1 3/4 feet.
- **Time.** You ran for 1/2 hour in the morning and 1/4 hour in the evening: 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 hour total, which is 45 minutes.

## Mini FAQs

**Can I use any common denominator, not the lowest one?**
Yes. For 1/3 + 1/4 you can just multiply the denominators: 3 × 4 = 12. For 5/6 + 3/4 you could use 6 × 4 = 24: 20/24 + 18/24 = 38/24 = 19/12. It works, but the numbers are bigger, so there's more to simplify at the end. The LCM keeps things tidy.

**Does the order matter?**
No. 1/3 + 1/4 is the same as 1/4 + 1/3. Both equal 7/12. Addition is commutative for fractions, just like whole numbers.

**Why can't I add the denominators?**
Because the denominator names the piece size. Adding 2/8 + 3/8 gives 5/8, five eighth-sized pieces. If you added the bottoms too, you'd get 5/16, which is a *smaller* amount than what you started with. That can't be right. Your eyes should catch that before your pencil does.
